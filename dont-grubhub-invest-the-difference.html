the $9 delivery fee is an investment (in someone else)

every delivery order is a small fee you barely notice and a bigger one you never see: the growth that money would've earned. here's what the habit actually costs over a decade.

6 min readupdated july 2026topic: spend smart

the short version

  • the average household spends about $329/month on food away from home. much of it is delivery you didn't quite decide on.
  • the visible cost is the fee stack — delivery fee + service fee + tip + menu markup. the invisible cost is the growth that money never earns.
  • redirecting just $165/month (half the average) into investments is about $335,000 over 30 years at historical returns.
  • this isn't 'never order takeout.' it's 'decide, don't default' — keep the orders you actually want, invest the rest.

the two costs of a delivery order

There are two prices on every delivery order. You see one of them.

The visible one is the stack: a delivery fee, a service fee, a small-order fee sometimes, a tip, and a menu that's often marked up 15–25% versus in-store. A $14 burrito quietly becomes $26. You know this one, even if you don't add it up.

The invisible one is what that money would have become. Every dollar spent on the avoidable version of convenience is a dollar that can't be invested — and over years, that forgone growth dwarfs the fees. That's the real cost, and nobody puts it on the receipt.

what the habit costs over time

The average U.S. household spends roughly $329 a month on food away from home (the detail is in eat well, spend less). Say half of that — $165 — is the avoided-decision kind: the 7pm order placed because deciding felt like too much, not because you wanted that specific meal.

the honest caveat

These figures assume ~9.5% average annual returns with dividends reinvested — the rough long-run historical average, not a promise. Real years swing wildly. The point isn't the exact number; it's the order of magnitude, which is genuinely large.

decide, don't default

This is the part where money advice usually turns preachy and tells you to never enjoy anything. That's not this.

Some delivery orders are worth every cent — the meal with a friend, the restaurant you love, the night you're genuinely too wiped to cook and the alternative is not eating. Those are the treat. Keep them, on purpose, guilt-free.

The target is only the default orders: the ones that happen because deciding what to eat at 7pm felt impossible. That's not a craving; it's the absence of an easier option. So build the easier option:

  • Two no-decision meals in the house at all times — something frozen, something shelf-stable, whatever you'd actually eat on your worst day.
  • Delete one delivery app from your phone. The friction of re-adding it is often enough to convert a default order back into a real decision.
  • Auto-invest the difference the same day, so the money you didn't spend actually goes somewhere instead of evaporating into other spending.

Knowing what it costs and choosing it anyway is the whole skill. The goal was never zero takeout. It was to stop spending — and un-investing — money you didn't decide to.

questions people actually ask

isn't my time worth something too?

Absolutely — and sometimes paying for delivery to reclaim an evening is a genuinely good trade. The distinction is whether you're buying back time you value or just avoiding a small decision. The first is worth it; the second is the expensive default this guide targets. Keep the trades you'd make on purpose.

i can't invest $165/month. is this pointless for me?

Not at all — scale it to reality. Even $25 or $50 a month redirected and invested consistently builds real money over time, and the habit matters more than the amount early on. The figures here are illustrative of the mechanism; use whatever number is actually true for you.

what about the delivery driver's tip?

Tip the driver well — that's a person doing work, and it's not where the savings should come from. The savings come from ordering delivery less often by default, not from under-tipping the orders you do place. Fewer orders, fully tipped, beats more orders tipped poorly.
