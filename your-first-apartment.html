take control · guide

your first apartment

the rent is the number they advertise. here's every other number — deposit, application fees, renters insurance, the true move-in total — and the lease clauses that quietly cost you.

8 min readupdated july 2026topic: take control

the short version

  • the old rule of thumb is spending under 30% of take-home pay on rent — useful as a target, though high-cost cities make it hard.
  • move-in costs way more than first month's rent: budget for security deposit + first (sometimes last) month + application/admin fees.
  • renters insurance is cheap (often ~$15/month) and covers your stuff plus liability — many landlords now require it.
  • read the lease for the expensive clauses: early-termination penalties, automatic renewals, who pays which utilities, and the move-out conditions for your deposit.

what you can actually afford

The classic guideline: keep rent under 30% of your take-home pay. On $3,500 a month after taxes, that's about $1,050 on rent.

Treat it as a target, not a law. In expensive metros it's often unrealistic, and plenty of people spend more by necessity — the honest move there is to know you're doing it and cut elsewhere on purpose, not to pretend the rule doesn't apply to you. (The full income-splitting framework is in split your paycheck.) The number that matters most isn't rent alone — it's rent plus the utilities and costs that come with the place, which is where first-timers get surprised.

the move-in cost nobody warns you about

The single biggest first-apartment shock is how much you need before you get the keys. It is never just first month's rent.

costtypicalnotes
first month's rent1x rentdue at signing
security deposit1–2x rentrefundable if you leave it clean
last month's rent0–1x rentsome landlords require it upfront
application & admin fees$25–$200+often non-refundable
utility deposits / setupvarieselectric, gas, internet

On a $1,200 apartment, "move in" can easily mean $3,000–$4,000 out the door before you've bought a single piece of furniture. Save that full number first; being short at signing is how people end up putting a deposit on a credit card at 22%.

renters insurance: cheap and worth it

Renters insurance is one of the best-value products in personal finance and one of the most skipped. For often around $15 a month, it covers two things:

  • Your stuff — if there's a fire, theft, or certain water damage, it pays to replace your belongings. Your landlord's insurance covers the building, not your things.
  • Liability — if someone's hurt in your place, or you accidentally cause damage, it can cover you.

Many landlords now require it, but get it even if yours doesn't. Replacing everything you own out of pocket after a disaster is exactly the kind of low-odds, high-cost event insurance exists for. (The logic of deductibles and coverage is the same as in health insurance, decoded.)

reading the lease for the traps

The lease is a contract, and the expensive parts are rarely the headline. Before you sign, find these:

  • Early termination. What happens if you need to leave early? Some leases charge two months' rent or make you liable until they re-rent. Know the number before you're stuck.
  • Automatic renewal. Many leases auto-renew unless you give notice (often 30–60 days) before the end. Miss the window and you can be locked in another year.
  • Utilities. Which are included, which are yours? "Heat included" versus "tenant pays all utilities" can be a $150/month difference the ad didn't mention.
  • Deposit return conditions. What counts as "normal wear" versus damage you pay for? Photograph everything at move-in and move-out — it's your evidence for getting the deposit back.

the move that protects your deposit

On day one, before you move anything in, take dated photos and a video of every room — every existing scuff, stain, and ding. Email them to yourself so they're timestamped. When you move out, do it again. That five-minute habit is the difference between getting your deposit back and arguing about it.

questions people actually ask

what if i don't have credit or a rental history yet?

Common for a first apartment, and there are standard workarounds: a co-signer or guarantor (often a parent), offering a larger deposit, showing proof of income (many landlords look for income around 3x the rent), or providing references. Ask the landlord directly what they'll accept — most have a process for first-time renters.

how much should i have saved before renting?

At minimum, the full move-in cost (first month + deposit + fees) plus a buffer — ideally a small emergency fund on top, so a surprise expense in month one doesn't sink you. Moving itself also costs money (truck, supplies, basic furniture), so pad your estimate.

is it worth getting a roommate?

Financially, almost always — splitting rent and utilities is the fastest way to get your housing cost under that 30% target and free up money for savings and debt. The trade-offs are personal (space, privacy, compatibility). If you do, put both names on the lease and agree in writing who pays what, to avoid the classic roommate money fights.

should i pay extra for a nicer place if i can technically afford it?

Remember that rent is a fixed cost you're locked into for a year — the hardest kind of spending to walk back. Every extra $100/month is $1,200 you can't redirect to an emergency fund, debt, or investing. A slightly less exciting apartment you can easily afford buys flexibility everywhere else in your budget.
