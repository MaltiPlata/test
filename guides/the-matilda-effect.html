the matilda effect

a woman describes a pattern in 1870. a historian names it after her in 1993. the pattern is women's work, quietly recredited to men — and it's the reason this whole project carries her name.

6 min readthe fine printwhy the name?

the short version

  • the Matilda Effect is the documented pattern of women's discoveries being ignored, minimized, or credited to male colleagues.
  • it's named for Matilda Joslyn Gage, a 19th-century suffragist who described the pattern in 1870 — and was herself largely written out of history.
  • historian Margaret Rossiter coined the term in 1993; it has since been cited in hundreds of studies.
  • this isn't only science history — it's the same erasure that shows up in whose financial expertise gets trusted, which is why maltiplata is named for her.

the woman who named the pattern that erased her

In 1870, an American suffragist named Matilda Joslyn Gage published an 1870 essay, "Woman as Inventor", pushing back on the widespread claim that women lacked inventive or scientific ability. She argued the opposite — that women had made such contributions all along, and that history simply declined to record them.

Gage worked alongside Susan B. Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and by many accounts was among the most radical thinkers of the movement. And then, in one of history's grimmer ironies, she was largely written out of it — remembered far less than her collaborators, her contributions absorbed and overshadowed. The woman who diagnosed the erasure of women became a case study in it.

the historian who made it stick

About a century later, Gage's pamphlet reached historian Margaret Rossiter, who had spent her career documenting forgotten women scientists. In a 1993 paper, Rossiter gave the pattern a name.

She was building on a known idea. In 1968, sociologist Robert K. Merton had coined the "Matthew Effect" — the tendency for already-famous scientists to accrue still more credit, while lesser-known collaborators get overlooked. Rossiter pointed out a gendered version: women's work, specifically, being reassigned to men. She named it the Matilda Effect, after Gage — deliberately restoring to Gage exactly the kind of credit the effect had denied her. As the Smithsonian profile of Rossiter puts it, she knew how to make a point stick: the term took off and has been cited in hundreds of studies since.

it's not ancient history

The examples are specific and famous once you look. Rosalind Franklin's X-ray work was central to discovering the structure of DNA; the Nobel went to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins. Lise Meitner helped explain nuclear fission; her collaborator Otto Hahn took the Nobel. Marie Curie's work was at times dismissed as merely assisting her husband. And a 2013 study found that both men and women rated identical research as stronger when they believed a man had done it — the bias operating in real time, not just in dusty archives.

the through-line to money

The Matilda Effect is usually told as science history, but the mechanism is broader: a systematic discounting of women's competence and contribution. That same discounting shows up in whose financial judgment gets trusted, who gets taken seriously in a negotiation, who's assumed to "not be a numbers person." The erasure and the money gap are branches of one root.

why we carry her name

maltiplata exists in the space this effect creates — the gap between what women are capable of and what the world set up for them to access. The name is a small act of the same thing Rossiter did: putting a woman's name back on the thing, on purpose.

"maltiplata" folds together Matilda and the Spanish plata — silver, money. A name for every woman written out of the record, placed back in the room, right next to the money she was owed. Not to shame anyone. To hand back what got quietly withheld: the words, the math, and the assumption of competence that should have been there all along.

That's the whole mission, compressed into a name. The rest of the site is just the details.

questions people actually ask

is the matilda effect actually proven, or is it a talking point?

It's a documented and studied phenomenon, not just rhetoric — Rossiter's naming built on extensive historical cases, and later controlled studies (like the 2013 research on how author gender changes perceived quality) found the bias operating measurably. Like any social science, individual cases can be debated, but the broad pattern is well-supported across decades of scholarship.

who was matilda joslyn gage, beyond the effect?

A leading 19th-century suffragist, abolitionist, and writer who edited the movement's official newspaper and co-authored major suffrage history alongside Anthony and Stanton. She was considered one of the movement's boldest theorists, particularly on the separation of church and state, and her relative obscurity today is itself often cited as an example of the effect that bears her name.

what does this have to do with my money?

The same undervaluing of women's competence that erased scientific credit also shows up in financial life — in who gets taken seriously asking for a raise, whose expertise is assumed, and who was never taught the money basics in the first place. maltiplata's whole premise is closing that access gap, which is why it's named for the woman who first described it.
