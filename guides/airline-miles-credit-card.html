spend smart · guide

travel cards, without the hype

'free flights' is the pitch. the truth is more useful: rewards cards are a rebate on spending you'd do anyway — worth real money if you pay in full, a trap if you don't.

6 min readupdated july 2026topic: spend smart

the short version

  • rewards are only 'free' if you pay the full balance every month. carry a balance at ~22% and the interest dwarfs any points.
  • a good travel card can be worth it — but do the annual fee math: the perks and points must clearly exceed the fee for your actual spending.
  • never spend more to earn points. a reward is a rebate on planned spending, not a reason to spend.
  • for most people, a simple flat cash-back card beats a complicated points card — no games, no expiring miles, no blackout dates.

the one rule that makes or breaks it

Before any discussion of points, one rule governs everything: if you carry a balance, rewards cards lose. Always.

Travel and rewards cards typically charge higher interest than plain cards, and the average credit card APR is around 22%. Earn 2% in points while paying 22% in interest and you're down 20%. No sign-up bonus survives that math. So the entire rewards conversation assumes one thing: you pay the full statement balance every month, every time. (Why that matters, in detail: the interest math nobody shows you.)

If that's you — a full-payer — then rewards are genuinely free money, and worth optimizing. If it's not yet, fix that first; the points aren't worth the trap.

the annual fee math

Many travel cards charge an annual fee — sometimes $95, sometimes $550+. The card is worth it only if what you get back clearly exceeds what you pay in. That's a calculation, not a vibe.

how to actually decide

Add up the concrete value you would use: the sign-up bonus (first year), the annual travel credits you'd actually spend, lounge access you'd actually use, plus the points you'd earn on your real spending. If that total beats the annual fee with room to spare, it may pay. If you're straining to justify the fee with perks you "might" use, it doesn't. Be honest about your actual habits, not your aspirational ones.

A no-annual-fee card is the safe default. A fee card is a bet that your specific travel and spending will more than earn it back — a bet that pays for some people and quietly loses for many who signed up for the marketing.

why points are trickier than cash

Airline and hotel points feel valuable, but they come with strings cash doesn't:

  • Variable value. A point isn't a fixed number of cents — its worth swings by how you redeem it, and programs can devalue points whenever they choose.
  • Restrictions. Blackout dates, limited award seats, transfer rules, and expiration can all stand between you and the "free flight."
  • Lock-in. Airline-branded cards tie you to one airline's ecosystem, which only pays off if that airline actually serves where you go.

This is why, for most people, a straightforward flat-rate cash-back card — say 2% on everything, no fee — often beats a complex points card. Cash has no blackout dates, never expires, and requires no strategy. The points hobby can be rewarding if you enjoy optimizing it; if you don't, simple cash back quietly wins.

questions people actually ask

will opening a card hurt my credit score?

A new card causes a small, temporary dip from the hard inquiry and lowers your average account age slightly — but it also raises your total available credit, which can help your utilization ratio over time. For a responsible full-payer, the long-term effect is usually neutral-to-positive. Opening many cards in a short window is what to avoid.

what's a sign-up bonus really worth?

It's the single biggest source of value on most travel cards — often worth several hundred dollars if you'd naturally hit the required spend. The trap is 'manufacturing' spending you wouldn't otherwise do to reach the bonus; if you have to stretch to hit it, the bonus can cost more than it gives. Only count it if you'd hit the spend anyway.

cash back or travel points for a beginner?

For most beginners, flat cash back. It's simple, flexible, never expires with blackout dates, and requires zero strategy — you can't 'do it wrong.' Travel points reward people who travel enough and enjoy optimizing redemptions. Start simple; graduate to points later only if the hobby actually appeals to you.
