spend smart · guide

buying your first car

the sticker price is the smallest decision. the loan rate, the term length, and the true cost of 'used' are where the real money is won or lost.

8 min readupdated july 2026topic: spend smart

the short version

  • in early 2026, average auto loan rates were about 6.4% new / 11.4% used — and your credit score can swing that by 10+ points.
  • dealers sell you a monthly payment; what matters is the total cost. a longer loan lowers the monthly but can cost thousands more in interest.
  • get pre-approved for financing at your bank or credit union first, then treat the dealer's offer as something to beat.
  • the price is just the start — insurance, gas, maintenance, registration are the ownership costs that break budgets.

how car financing actually works

Most people don't buy a car; they buy a loan attached to a car. So the loan is where to focus.

Your interest rate is set largely by your credit score. In early 2026, Experian put average new-car loans around 6.4% and used around 11.4% — but that's an average. Excellent credit might see ~4.5%; poor credit can mean 16% or more. On a $30,000 car over five years, that spread is real money:

ratemonthly (60 mo)total interest
6.4% (good credit, new)$585$5,126
11.4% (used / fair credit)$659$9,523
16% (poor credit)$730$13,782

Same car. The interest rate alone can cost or save you $8,000+. Which is why improving your credit before you buy, and shopping the rate, matters as much as haggling the price.

the monthly-payment trick

The most important number the dealer will try to steer you away from is the total cost. Their favorite tool is the monthly payment.

Here's the trick: any car can hit any monthly payment if you stretch the loan long enough. A 72- or 84-month loan makes an unaffordable car look affordable — but you pay far more interest, and you spend years "underwater," owing more than the car is worth.

the move

Negotiate the total price of the car, not the monthly payment. When a salesperson asks "what monthly payment are you looking for?", redirect: "let's agree on the out-the-door price first." And keep the loan term as short as you can afford — ideally 60 months or less. A payment you can cover on a shorter term is a car you can actually afford.

new vs. used vs. certified

A new car loses a big chunk of its value the moment you drive it off the lot — often 20%+ in the first year. That depreciation is real money you're paying for the new-car smell.

  • New: lowest interest rates, full warranty, highest depreciation. You pay a premium to be the first owner.
  • Used: someone else ate the worst depreciation, so more car for the money — but higher interest rates and more unknowns.
  • Certified pre-owned (CPO): a middle path — a used car (often a 3-year-old lease return) inspected and warrantied by the manufacturer, sometimes with special financing.

For a first car, a well-chosen used or CPO vehicle is often the value sweet spot: you skip the steepest depreciation without taking on the full risk of a private-sale used car.

the costs after you buy

The purchase price is the beginning of the spending, not the end. Budget for the ownership stack before you commit:

  • Insurance — required, and it varies a lot by car, age, and location. Get quotes on the specific car before buying; a cheap car with expensive insurance isn't cheap.
  • Gas / charging — a fuel-efficient car can save thousands over years.
  • Maintenance and repairs — oil, tires, brakes, the surprise $800 thing. Keep a small car fund so a repair doesn't go on a credit card.
  • Registration, taxes, fees — ongoing and often underestimated.

One 2026 note worth knowing: a recent federal law created a temporary deduction of up to $10,000/year in auto-loan interest on new, U.S.-assembled vehicles — but treat that as a possible bonus to confirm with a tax resource, not a reason to buy more car than you need.

questions people actually ask

should i pay cash or finance?

If you can pay cash without draining your emergency fund, it removes interest entirely — a guaranteed 'return' equal to the loan rate. But don't empty your safety net to do it; being car-rich and cash-poor is its own trap. A middle path: a solid down payment (which lowers your rate and interest) plus a short loan you can comfortably cover.

how much should i put down?

A larger down payment lowers your loan amount, your interest, and often your rate, and keeps you from going underwater. A common target is around 20% on a new car, less on used — but the real answer is: enough that the loan term stays short and the payment fits your budget without straining the rest of it.

is a car loan 'good debt'?

It's neither good nor bad by default — it's a tool. A modest loan at a low rate for a reliable car you need for work is reasonable. A long loan at a high rate for more car than you need is the version that hurts. The rate and the term decide which one you're in, more than the fact of borrowing itself.

new car vs. keeping my beater running?

Run the math honestly: a paid-off older car usually wins financially, even with occasional repairs, until repairs start rivaling a car payment or reliability becomes a safety/work problem. 'It needs a $600 repair' is almost always cheaper than a $585/month loan. Replace it when the numbers or your safety genuinely say so, not for the upgrade.
