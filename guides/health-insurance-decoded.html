health insurance, decoded

premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket max — five words that decide what a hospital visit costs you. how they actually fit together, in order.

8 min readupdated july 2026topic: take control

the short version

  • the five words in order: premium (monthly, always) → deductible (you pay first) → copay/coinsurance (you split) → out-of-pocket max (the ceiling).
  • the out-of-pocket maximum is the number that protects you from catastrophe: for 2026, in-network care is capped around $10,600 for an individual. after that, the plan pays 100%.
  • a low premium usually means a high deductible, and vice versa. the 'cheap' plan isn't cheap if you actually use it.
  • preventive care — checkups, screenings, vaccines — is $0 on ACA-compliant plans, even before you hit your deductible. use it.

why this feels deliberately confusing

Health insurance uses five key words, and almost nobody explains how they connect. So you end up nodding along at open enrollment, picking a plan half-blind, and discovering what it actually covers only when you're already sick and holding a bill. Let's fix the order once.

The whole system is really a sequence: who pays, in what order, up to what limit. Learn the sequence and every plan document suddenly reads clearly.

1. premium — the price of having it at all

Your premium is the fixed amount you pay every month to keep the insurance active — whether or not you go to a single appointment. If you get insurance through work, your employer usually pays a big chunk and your share comes out of your paycheck (often pre-tax, which lowers your taxable income — see why is my paycheck smaller than my salary).

Key mental model: the premium is what you pay to be in the game. Everything else below is what you pay when you actually use care. And there's a seesaw between them — a lower premium almost always means you pay more when you use the plan.

2. deductible — what you pay before it kicks in

Your deductible is the amount you pay out of pocket for covered care before your insurance starts chipping in. On a $2,000-deductible plan, you pay the first $2,000 of (non-preventive) covered care yourself. Until you hit it, the insurer is mostly watching from the sidelines.

This is the number that makes a "cheap" plan expensive. A low monthly premium paired with a $7,000 deductible means that if something goes wrong, you're paying a lot before help arrives.

the myth to drop

"I have to pay the sticker price until I hit my deductible." False. Even during the deductible phase, you pay the insurer's negotiated rate, which is often far lower than the hospital's list price. Always look at the Explanation of Benefits (the "EOB," not a bill) for the real allowed amount.

3. copay & coinsurance — the shared middle

Once you've met your deductible, you and your insurer split costs. This happens two ways:

  • Copay — a flat fee per service. $30 for a doctor visit, $15 for a generic prescription. Predictable, and some copays (like primary care) may apply even before you've met your deductible.
  • Coinsurance — a percentage of the cost. "20% coinsurance" means after the deductible, you pay 20% of a covered bill and the plan pays 80%. On a $1,200 MRI, that's $240.

So the flow so far: you pay premiums always, you pay the full negotiated cost until the deductible is met, then you pay copays/coinsurance while the plan covers the rest. Which leaves one question — is there any ceiling? Yes, and it's the most important number of all.

4. out-of-pocket maximum — the number that saves you

The out-of-pocket maximum is the most you'll pay for covered, in-network care in a plan year. Once your deductible + copays + coinsurance add up to this number, your insurance pays 100% of covered care for the rest of the year.

This is the whole reason insurance exists — the cap that turns a $150,000 hospital bill into a known, survivable number. Federal law caps it for ACA-compliant plans; per $10,600 for an individual in 2026 (and $21,200 for a family), with high-deductible plans capped lower.

what the plan pays after you hit it

Cigna's worked example makes it concrete: with a $6,350 out-of-pocket max, a surprise $150,000 surgery-and-hospital-stay costs you $6,350, not $150,000. That gap is the product you're actually buying. Premiums do not count toward this max — only what you spend on care does.

putting it together: choosing a plan

Now the five words become a decision. The core trade-off is premium vs. deductible, and the right answer depends on how much care you expect to use.

rarely see a doctor, generally healthylower premium, higher deductibleyou save monthly; the high out-of-pocket max still caps a disaster
have regular prescriptions, appointments, or a planned procedurehigher premium, lower deductiblepredictable costs; help kicks in sooner

Two things almost everyone should do regardless of plan: use your free preventive care (ACA-compliant plans cover checkups, screenings, and vaccines at $0, even before the deductible), and stay in-network whenever possible, since out-of-network care can cost far more and sometimes doesn't count toward your out-of-pocket max at all.

The official plain-language reference for all of this is HealthCare.gov, which is worth a look before any open enrollment.

questions people actually ask

what's an HSA and should i have one?

A Health Savings Account is a tax-advantaged account you can use only if you have a qualifying high-deductible health plan (per the IRS's annual rules). It's unusually powerful: money goes in pre-tax, grows tax-free, and comes out tax-free for medical costs — a rare triple advantage. If you're healthy, have an HDHP, and can afford to leave the money invested, an HSA doubles as a stealth retirement account. See where to actually put your money.

what's the difference between an HSA and an FSA?

Both let you pay for medical costs with pre-tax money, but an HSA (paired with a high-deductible plan) rolls over year to year and is yours to keep and invest forever. A traditional FSA is more 'use it or lose it' within the year and is tied to your employer. HSA is a long-term asset; FSA is a short-term budgeting tool.

in-network vs. out-of-network — how much does it matter?

A lot. Insurers negotiate lower rates with in-network providers, and your deductible, copays, and out-of-pocket max are built around those. Go out-of-network and you may face a separate, higher deductible, higher coinsurance, and — critically — costs that sometimes don't count toward your out-of-pocket cap at all. Always check that a provider is in-network before a non-emergency visit.

i'm healthy — why not just take the cheapest plan?

You can, and for many healthy people a low-premium/high-deductible plan is genuinely the smart pick — the out-of-pocket max still protects you from catastrophe. The thing to confirm first: could you actually cover that deductible if you had to tomorrow? If a surprise $5,000–$7,000 bill would be impossible, the 'cheap' plan carries a risk the pricier one removes. Match the plan to your savings, not just your health.
