where to actually put your money

the order of accounts almost nobody explains: where the first dollar goes, where the next one goes, and why starting ten years earlier is worth more than earning more.

9 min readupdated july 2026topic: grow

the short version

  • the order that matters: employer match → high-rate debt → emergency fund → Roth IRA → taxable brokerage. skip a step and you leave the most valuable dollars on the table.
  • a 401(k) match is a 100% return, instantly. nothing else on this list competes, so it goes first even before debt.
  • starting at 25 instead of 35 — same $200/month — is worth about $680,000 more by 65. the ten years is doing more work than the money.
  • for 2026 you can put up to $7,500 in an IRA (under 50). you don't need to hit that. you need to start.

why this feels harder than it is

Investing has a branding problem. It's wrapped in jargon, ticker symbols, and men on television yelling about stocks, and the whole aesthetic seems designed to make you feel like you need permission to start. You don't. The actual mechanics fit on an index card.

Here's the uncomfortable truth the complexity hides: the single biggest factor in how much money you end up with isn't which fund you pick or whether you time the market. It's when you start. And every year spent feeling not-ready-yet is the one input you can never get back.

So this guide is an order of operations, not a stock tip. Do these in sequence. Each step is "good enough" before the next one matters at all.

step 1: the free money (employer match)

If your job offers a 401(k) match, that is the first place your money goes — before debt, before anything.

A typical match is something like "we'll match 100% of what you put in, up to 3% of your salary." Read that carefully: if you contribute 3%, your employer adds another 3%. That's a 100% return on your money, the instant it lands. There is no investment on earth that reliably does that. Not paying off a 22% credit card, not anything.

the move

Find out your employer's match (HR, or your benefits portal) and contribute at least enough to get all of it. Anything less is declining part of your salary. If there's no match, skip straight to step 2 — you'll come back to the 401(k) later.

steps 2 & 3: clear the way

Before you invest beyond the match, two things go first, because they earn a guaranteed return that beating the market can't promise.

step 2: kill high-rate debt

Paying off a card at 22% is a guaranteed, tax-free 22% return. The stock market has historically averaged around 10% a year — so mathematically, clearing high-rate debt beats investing almost every time. (The full plan is in how to get out of debt.)

step 3: a real emergency fund

Three to six months of essential expenses, in a high-yield savings account — not invested. This is the thing that keeps a job loss or medical bill from forcing you to sell investments at the worst possible moment, or reach for a card. It's boring and it's load-bearing.

step 4: the roth ira (your main character)

Now you invest. For most people starting out, the best account is a Roth IRA.

A Roth IRA is an account you open yourself — not through work — and fund with money you've already paid tax on. In exchange, it grows tax-free and you pay nothing when you withdraw in retirement. For someone early in their career, likely in a lower tax bracket now than later, that trade is usually excellent.

For 2026, the IRS set the 2026 limit at $7,500 a year if you're under 50 (across all your IRAs combined). You do not need to hit that. $50 a month is a real start. But note income limits apply to the Roth: single filers with income under $153,000 can contribute the full amount, phasing out above that.

what to actually buy inside it

Opening the account is not the same as investing — cash sitting in a Roth does nothing. Once it's open, buy something. For most people the boring answer is the right one: a low-cost, broad index fund (see stocks aren't all the same thing for what that means). Set an automatic monthly contribution and let it run.

why starting now beats starting bigger

This is the part that should make you a little angry nobody showed you sooner.

what the ten-year head start is worth

The person who started earlier put in only $24,000 more, and ended up with roughly $680,000 more. That gap isn't the contributions — it's four extra decades of the earnings themselves earning. That's compounding, and it's the entire reason "start now, small" beats "start later, bigger."

A caveat on the numbers: these assume a long-run average return around 9.5% with dividends reinvested, which is roughly what the S&P 500 has done historically per Fidelity. Real years are nothing like an average — some are up 26%, some down 18%. The average is a simple average of good years and bad, and it only shows up if you stay in through the bad ones.

step 5: everything after

Maxed the Roth and still have money to invest? Two good next steps, in either order: go back and raise your 401(k) contribution past the match (up toward its own limit), or open a regular taxable brokerage account.

A taxable brokerage has no contribution limit and no withdrawal rules — you can take the money out anytime. The trade-off is no special tax treatment: you owe tax on gains when you sell. It's the right home for money you're investing for goals beyond retirement, or simply for more than the tax-advantaged accounts can hold.

Same principle inside it as everywhere else: low-cost index funds, automatic contributions, left alone.

the two mistakes that actually cost you

Not fund selection. These two:

Waiting to feel ready. You've now seen the price of a ten-year delay. There is no version of the math where waiting wins. Start with an amount so small it's almost silly, so that "I can't afford to" stops being the reason.

Panic-selling in a downturn. The market will drop, sometimes hard, and every instinct will scream to sell. Selling in a crash is how you turn a temporary paper loss into a permanent real one. The historical average return already includes every crash — it's the reward for not flinching. Automating your contributions helps here too: it keeps you buying when prices are low, which is exactly when it feels worst and matters most.

questions people actually ask

what if my job doesn't offer a 401(k)?

Then you start at the Roth IRA — you open it yourself through any major brokerage, no employer needed. The 401(k) match is only 'step 1' when it exists; no match means no free money to grab first, so you move straight to clearing high-rate debt and building the emergency fund, then the Roth.

roth or traditional — how do i choose?

Rough rule: if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket later than you are now — which is true for most people early in their careers — Roth usually wins, because you pay the tax now at the lower rate and never again.

Traditional makes more sense if you're a high earner today expecting lower income in retirement. When genuinely unsure, Roth's flexibility (you can withdraw your own contributions, not earnings, without penalty) makes it the safer default for a first account.

how much do i really need to start?

Many brokerages now let you start with no minimum and buy fractional shares, so functionally: whatever you have. $25 a month invested consistently starting today beats $500 a month you keep planning to start 'once things settle down.'

is now a bad time to start because the market is high/low?

The honest answer is that nobody reliably knows whether today is a high or a low until years later, and people who wait for the 'right' moment tend to miss years of growth waiting. Investing a fixed amount on a schedule regardless of price sidesteps the question entirely — you buy more shares when it's cheap and fewer when it's expensive, automatically.
