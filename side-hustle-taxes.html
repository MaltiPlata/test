side hustle taxes, without the panic

the myth is you don't owe until $600. the real number is $400 — and the IRS gets the data whether or not a form shows up. here's how to stay ahead of it.

7 min readupdated july 2026topic: earn

the short version

  • the real threshold is $400 in net self-employment income — not $600. above it, you owe self-employment tax whether or not you get a 1099.
  • self-employment tax is 15.3% (Social Security + Medicare), on top of regular income tax — because you're now paying both the employee and employer halves.
  • set aside ~25–30% of side-hustle profit for taxes as you earn, and pay quarterly estimates if you'll owe $1,000+.
  • deductions reduce what you owe — track business expenses (supplies, mileage, home office, software) all year, not at filing time.

the $600 myth, corrected

Here's the misconception that costs side hustlers money every year: "I don't owe taxes until I make $600." Wrong number, wrong concept.

The $600 (recently in flux and changing again) is a reporting threshold — it determines when a platform must send you a 1099 form. The tax threshold is different and lower: per IRS rules, if your net self-employment income (what's left after business expenses) is the $400 threshold or more, you owe self-employment tax — even if no form ever arrives.

And the IRS increasingly gets the data anyway, matching payment-app records and bank deposits. "No form" has never meant "no tax." The safe assumption: every dollar of profit is reportable, and above $400 net it's taxable.

which taxes you actually owe

As a side hustler you're now, in the IRS's eyes, a business. That means two layers of tax:

  • Self-employment tax — 15.3%. This is Social Security (12.4%) and Medicare (2.9%). As an employee, your employer pays half of this for you invisibly; self-employed, you pay both halves. (The employee side of this is explained in why is my paycheck smaller than my salary.) One relief: you can deduct half of it.
  • Regular income tax. Your side-hustle profit stacks on top of your other income and is taxed at your normal marginal rate.

The combination is why a good rule of thumb is to set aside roughly 25–30% of your net side-hustle income for taxes — the moment the money comes in, not at filing time.

quarterly taxes (the part that surprises people)

W-2 jobs withhold taxes from every paycheck automatically. Side hustles don't — so the IRS expects you to pay as you go, yourself, four times a year.

If you expect to owe $1,000 or more in tax for the year, you generally need to make quarterly estimated payments (Form 1040-ES), due roughly in April, June, September, and January. Skip them and you can owe an underpayment penalty even if you pay in full at tax time.

the system that keeps it painless

Open a separate savings account for taxes. Every time side-hustle money lands, immediately move ~30% into it. When quarterly payments are due, the money is already there — you never feel a shortfall, and there's no April panic. This one habit prevents the single worst side-hustle tax experience: owing money you already spent.

deductions: the part that saves you money

Here's the upside. Because you're taxed on net income (profit after expenses), legitimate business costs directly lower your tax bill. Common ones:

  • Supplies and equipment used for the hustle
  • Mileage driven for the business (tracked, at the IRS standard rate)
  • Home office — a portion of rent/utilities if you have dedicated workspace (a simplified method exists)
  • Software, subscriptions, and fees the business requires
  • A share of phone and internet used for work

The catch: you need records. Keep receipts, log mileage, and separate business spending from personal — ideally a dedicated card or account for the hustle. Reconstructing this at tax time is miserable and error-prone; capturing it as you go is easy and often uncovers deductions you'd have missed.

Because tax rules shift (the 1099 thresholds have changed repeatedly), verify current specifics at IRS.gov's Self-Employed Tax Center or with a tax professional — especially your first year.

questions people actually ask

do i need an LLC for my side hustle?

Not to start — by default a solo side hustle is a 'sole proprietorship,' and you report it on Schedule C with your personal taxes, no formation required. An LLC can offer liability protection and is worth considering as things grow, but plenty of profitable side hustles run for years as sole proprietorships. Don't let 'I need to form a company first' become a reason to delay.

what if my side hustle loses money?

If it's a genuine business (run to make a profit), a loss can sometimes offset other income — but the IRS distinguishes a business from a hobby, and hobby losses generally aren't deductible. Keep records showing you're operating like a real business. If losses persist, that's worth a conversation with a tax pro about how the IRS views it.

how do i pay the quarterly taxes, practically?

Online through IRS Direct Pay or the EFTPS system, or by mail with Form 1040-ES vouchers. You estimate the amount based on expected profit (many people use last year's numbers as a starting point). If you also have a W-2 job, an alternative is to increase withholding there to cover the side-hustle tax, avoiding quarterly payments entirely.

i've earned side income for years and never reported it. now what?

The practical answer is to start doing it correctly going forward and consider talking to a tax professional about prior years — the IRS generally prefers people who come into compliance, and there are processes for amending past returns. This is genuinely a case for professional advice rather than a guide; the specifics of your situation matter a lot.
