the longevity gap

women live longer — which sounds like good news until you do the retirement math. more years, often less saved, sometimes alone. the gap nobody plans for.

6 min readthe fine printgrow

the short version

  • women live longer than men on average — so their retirement savings need to stretch across more years.
  • that longer horizon lands on top of typically lower lifetime earnings and less investing — three gaps compounding into one retirement shortfall.
  • career breaks for caregiving often mean fewer years contributing and smaller Social Security and retirement balances.
  • the response is structural: invest earlier and more aggressively for the long horizon, and treat retirement saving as non-negotiable, not optional.

living longer is a financial event

Women, on average, outlive men by several years. In almost every context that's straightforwardly good news — more life. But in retirement planning specifically, it creates a challenge nobody warns you about: your money has to last longer than a man's would.

More years in retirement means more years of expenses — housing, food, and especially healthcare, which rises with age — funded by savings that stopped growing from new contributions when you retired. A retirement that needs to cover, say, 30 years instead of 25 requires meaningfully more money to begin with. That's the longevity gap: the same lifespan advantage that's a gift in life is a funding problem in retirement, unless you plan for it.

the three gaps that compound

The longevity gap rarely arrives alone. It stacks on top of two others this series has covered, and together they form a single, compounding disadvantage:

gapeffect on retirement
wage gapless lifetime income to save from
investing gapmore of what's saved sits in cash, growing slowly
longevity gapthe (smaller) nest egg must last more years

Earn less, invest less of it, and need it to last longer. Any one is manageable; together they explain why women face a higher risk of running short in later life. Add career breaks for caregiving — which mean fewer years contributing to retirement accounts and often lower Social Security benefits (calculated from your earnings history) — and the challenge is structural, not personal.

planning for a longer horizon

Naming the gap points straight at the response: if your money needs to last longer and start from less, then starting earlier and investing for growth matters even more for you than the generic advice assumes.

  • Treat retirement saving as non-negotiable. It's the 'future-you' bucket in split your paycheck — the one that never gets cut to zero, precisely because you'll need it longest.
  • Invest for the long horizon. A longer life is a longer investing runway too — which is an argument for growth-oriented, equity-heavy investing over decades, not against it. (See where to actually put your money.)
  • Don't fully pause retirement during career breaks if you can help it — even small continued contributions, or a spousal IRA, keep the compounding alive through years out of the workforce.
  • Factor in healthcare. Later-life medical costs are significant; an HSA, if you're eligible, is a powerful long-term tool (see health insurance, decoded).

the reframe

A longer life isn't the problem — under-planning for it is. The extra years are a gift; the task is simply to fund them, and the single most powerful lever is time in the market. The woman who internalizes the longevity gap at 25 and invests accordingly turns her lifespan advantage back into what it should be: more good years, paid for.

a note on social security and structures

Some of this gap is beyond individual control, and it's worth saying so plainly. Social Security benefits are based on lifetime earnings, so the wage gap and caregiving breaks flow through into lower benefits. Survivor benefits, spousal benefits, and how couples coordinate claiming can matter a great deal — and are worth understanding (or getting advice on) rather than leaving to chance.

maltiplata's job is to make the personal levers visible: invest earlier, invest for growth, protect the retirement bucket, keep contributing through breaks where possible. The structural levers — pay equity, caregiving support, policy — are a longer fight. Both are real; only one is something you can act on this month.

questions people actually ask

exactly how many more years do women need to fund?

It varies by data source and cohort, but women's average life expectancy runs several years longer than men's, and the practical planning takeaway is to assume your retirement could last into your 90s. Rather than anchor to one number, plan for a long horizon — running short because you under-estimated longevity is the costly error to avoid.

does this mean women should invest more conservatively as they age?

Somewhat, but often less conservatively than instinct suggests — a longer lifespan means a longer investing horizon, so shifting entirely to cash/bonds too early can leave you exposed to inflation over 30 retirement years. The common approach is a gradual glide toward more stability approaching and during retirement, while keeping meaningful growth exposure because the money still needs to last decades.

what if i took years off for caregiving?

You're not out of options. Strategies include contributing to a spousal IRA during non-earning years, increasing contributions when you return to work, understanding your Social Security spousal/survivor options, and simply starting or resuming investing as soon as you can. The gap from a career break is real, but it's partially recoverable — and ignoring it is the only response that guarantees it stays.

where these numbers come from

  1. Women's longer average life expectancy is well-established in U.S. mortality data (CDC/SSA); the planning implication is a longer retirement funding horizon.
  2. Interaction with the wage and investing gaps draws on the sourcing in maltiplata's "taught to save, not to invest."
  3. Social Security benefits are based on lifetime earnings history; see ssa.gov for how spousal and survivor benefits work.
