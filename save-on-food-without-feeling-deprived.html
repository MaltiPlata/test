spend smart · guide

eat well, spend less

takeout is the tax, not the treat. what the average household actually spends on food — and the system that moves a few hundred dollars a month without anyone eating sad salads.

8 min readupdated july 2026topic: spend smart

the short version

  • in 2024 the average U.S. household spent $6,224 on groceries and $3,945 on food away from home — takeout and delivery included.
  • that's roughly $329 a month on food someone else cooked. it's the single most reachable line in most budgets.
  • the USDA publishes four grocery-cost tiers, updated monthly. use them as a benchmark instead of guessing whether you're overspending.
  • the system that works isn't meal prep. it's anchor meals, cook-once, and one honest look at your delivery history.

what people actually spend

Food is the third-biggest line in the average household budget, after housing and getting around. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, in 2024 the average household spent $6,224 on food at home and $3,945 on food away from home — a category that includes restaurants, takeout, and delivery.

Break that second number down and it's about $329 a month. Not one big indulgence — a hundred small ones, each of which felt completely reasonable at the time.

Nationally the trend is the same. The USDA Economic Research Service found total food spending hit $2.58 trillion in 2024, with the growth driven mostly by food away from home. We are all, collectively, paying more people to cook for us.

I want to be careful here, because this is where most money advice gets insufferable. Nobody is about to tell you that coffee is why you can't afford a house. It isn't. Housing was 33.4% of average household spending in 2024, per its 2024 release — the structural stuff dwarfs the lattes, and pretending otherwise is how people end up blaming themselves for prices they don't set.

But food away from home is different from rent in one specific, useful way: it's a line you have real leverage over, this month, without moving apartments or changing jobs. That's the whole reason it's worth attention. Not because it's your fault. Because it's reachable.

find out if you're actually overspending

Before changing anything, get a number. Most people have no idea whether their grocery spending is high, low, or completely normal — which makes it impossible to tell whether a "food problem" even exists.

Two ways to benchmark:

1. Your own history. Open your banking app and add up last month's grocery stores. Then add up the delivery apps and restaurants, separately. Two numbers. Most people are surprised by exactly one of them.

2. The USDA's tiers. The USDA maintains four official food plans — Thrifty, Low-Cost, Moderate, and Liberal — that estimate what a nutritionally adequate diet costs at four different comfort levels, assuming all meals are made at home. They're republished every month and adjusted for food-price inflation, and they break down by household size and age, so you can find something close to your situation.

how to read them

The Thrifty plan is the floor — it's the basis for SNAP benefits, and sustaining it takes real planning and almost no waste. Moderate is what a typical household lands near. If your grocery number sits well above Liberal, there's room. If you're at or under Thrifty, your grocery bill is not your problem and you can stop worrying about it.

One caveat worth knowing: every tier assumes you cook everything at home. So they're a grocery benchmark, not a food-spending benchmark. Your takeout is on top.

the system (it isn't meal prep)

Sunday meal prep works for a specific kind of person, and if you're not that person, failing at it repeatedly just teaches you that you're bad at this. You're not. The plan was wrong.

Here's what actually holds up.

anchor meals: pick five, not thirty

Decision fatigue is what drives the 6pm delivery order, not hunger. So shrink the decisions. Choose five meals you genuinely like, can cook mostly from memory, and that share ingredients. That's your rotation. You are not running a restaurant; you do not need variety every night, and you almost certainly didn't have it growing up either.

Shared ingredients is the part people skip. If four of your five meals use onions, garlic, rice, and some kind of bean, your grocery list gets shorter, your bulk buying gets cheaper, and your produce stops rotting in the drawer.

cook once, eat twice

Not "prep twelve identical containers on Sunday." Just: when you cook, make enough for two meals. Tonight's dinner is tomorrow's lunch. That single habit is the highest-leverage change on this list, because lunch bought out is the most consistently expensive meal of the week and the one you're least likely to have planned for.

the audit nobody wants to do

Open your delivery app and look at the order history. All of it. Then answer one question per order: was this a meal I wanted, or a decision I avoided?

Some will be the first kind — dinner with a friend, the place you love, food that was actually the point. Keep those. Those are the treat.

The rest are the tax. A $9 delivery fee plus a service fee plus a tip plus the menu markup, so that you didn't have to decide anything at 7pm on a Tuesday. That's what you're actually buying, and it's worth knowing the price.

the swap that does the most work

Keep two "no-decision" dinners in the house at all times — something frozen, something shelf-stable, whatever you'd actually eat on your worst day. The delivery order usually isn't a craving. It's the absence of an easier option.

what this is worth in a year

Say the audit shows you're at the national average for food away from home — about $329 a month — and half of it turns out to be the avoided-decision kind rather than the meals you actually wanted.

Cutting that in half is roughly $165 a month, or $1,980 a year. Some of it comes back as a higher grocery bill, because you're cooking more — call it a real saving of $1,200 to $1,500.

That's not a life-changing number and I'm not going to dress it up as one. But it's an emergency fund most of the way built, or several months knocked off a payoff date, from a change that involves nobody eating anything they hate.

And if you'd rather spend it on takeout — genuinely, with the number in front of you, having decided — then spend it on takeout. Knowing what it costs and choosing it anyway is the entire skill. That's a budget. The goal was never to spend less on food. It was to stop spending money you didn't decide to spend.

questions people actually ask

is cooking at home always cheaper?

Per meal, almost always — you're not paying for labor, rent, delivery, or markup. But it's not free, and the USDA's own plans assume real planning to hit the lower tiers.

Where it stops being cheaper is waste. Groceries you buy and then throw out are the most expensive food there is, because you paid full price for zero meals. That's the actual argument for a short, repeating rotation over an ambitious one.

what's a normal grocery budget for one person?

The USDA publishes exactly this, by age and sex, across its four cost tiers, and updates it monthly for inflation. Look up your own row rather than trusting a number from a blog — including this one — because the figures move with food prices and vary a lot by household size.

Single-person households also get a cost adjustment, since you lose the economies of scale a bigger household gets.

i have almost no time. what's the one thing?

Double whatever you cook. Nothing else on this list survives a genuinely packed week, and that one does, because it adds about four minutes to something you were already doing.

does this work if food is how i cope?

Sometimes food spending is doing a job that isn't about food — comfort, control, celebration, getting through a hard stretch. That's very human and no budgeting system addresses it.

If that's what's going on, a spreadsheet is the wrong tool and adding shame to it makes it worse. Be gentle about that part, and if it feels bigger than a habit, it's worth talking to someone about the underlying thing rather than the grocery line.
