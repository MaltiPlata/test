student loans, decoded

the rules changed more in the last year than in the decade before it. what's federal vs. private, what happened to SAVE, and how to find your actual payment without spiraling.

9 min readupdated july 2026topic: take control

the short version

  • this is a fast-moving area: SAVE was struck down, and new plans (RAP and a Tiered Standard) launched July 1, 2026. always confirm your specifics at StudentAid.gov.
  • know which you have: federal loans come with income-driven plans, forgiveness options, and protections; private loans mostly don't.
  • current federal rates are around 6.39% for undergraduates, fixed for the life of the loan; enrolling in auto-pay shaves off 1%.
  • if payments feel impossible, the answer is almost never 'ignore it' — an income-driven plan can drop the payment, sometimes dramatically, based on what you earn.

first: this ground is still moving

Student loans are, right now, the least stable topic in personal finance. In the space of about a year, a major repayment plan was struck down in court and Congress passed a law creating new ones. So before anything else, one durable piece of advice: verify your own situation at StudentAid.gov, the official federal site, rather than trusting any article — including this one — for the live specifics. This guide explains the concepts that don't change. The numbers and plan names, you confirm at the source.

With that said, here's the map.

federal vs. private: the most important distinction

The first thing to know about any student loan is which of two very different worlds it lives in.

federal loansprivate loans
who lendsthe U.S. governmentbanks, credit unions, online lenders
interest ratefixed, set by lawfixed or variable, based on credit
income-driven plansyesalmost never
forgiveness optionsyes (PSLF, IDR forgiveness)no
pause for hardshipdeferment / forbearancelimited, lender's discretion

The takeaway: federal loans come wrapped in borrower protections that private loans generally don't have. This is why the standard advice is to exhaust federal options before private ones, and why refinancing federal loans into a private loan — even at a lower rate — permanently gives up those protections. Sometimes that trade is worth it; often it isn't. Know what you're trading.

Not sure which you have? StudentAid.gov lists every federal loan under "My Aid." Anything not shown there is private, and you'd find it on your credit report or with the lender.

what they cost

Federal student loan rates are reset each year based on Treasury auctions and are fixed for the life of the loan — the rate you get at disbursement never changes. For recent undergraduate loans that's around 6.39% for undergraduate loans, with graduate and PLUS loans higher.

One easy, real saving: the a 1% auto-pay interest reduction for enrolling in automatic payments. On a typical balance that's meaningful money over the life of the loan, for the effort of setting up autopay once. If your loans are federal and not on autopay, that's a five-minute win.

the interest trap to avoid

When a loan is unsubsidized (most are), interest accrues even while you're in school or in a pause. If you don't pay it, it can capitalize — get added to your principal, so you start paying interest on interest. Paying even small amounts toward interest during school or a forbearance keeps the balance from quietly growing.

repayment plans (and what happened to SAVE)

Federal loans offer several repayment plans, and this is the part that changed most.

The SAVE plan — the most generous income-driven plan, with $0 payments for some low-income borrowers — was challenged in court and ultimately struck down, and the Department of Education moved affected borrowers off its benefits, with interest resuming in August 2025. In its place, a new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) and a Tiered Standard plan became available July 1, 2026.

Rather than give you numbers that may shift again, here's the framework that stays true:

  • Standard / Tiered Standard plans — fixed monthly payments, no income documentation needed. Predictable; you pay it off in a set term.
  • Income-driven plans (IDR) — your payment is tied to your income and family size, and after a set number of years, remaining balances can be forgiven. Payments can drop a lot if you earn little relative to what you owe.

You can switch plans at any time, for free, at StudentAid.gov. Which is the single most important thing to know if you're struggling.

if you can't make the payment

The worst thing you can do with a federal student loan is nothing. Missed payments lead to delinquency and eventually default, which wrecks your credit and can lead to wages being garnished — and none of it makes the balance smaller. But federal loans give you real off-ramps before that happens, and using them is not a failure.

  • Switch to an income-driven plan. If your income is low relative to your balance, your payment could drop substantially — sometimes to a very small number. This is the first lever to pull.
  • Deferment or forbearance. A temporary, sanctioned pause for hardship, unemployment, or school. Interest usually still accrues, so it's a bridge, not a fix — but it's infinitely better than defaulting.

the move

If you're scared of the payment, log in to StudentAid.gov and use the loan simulator, or call your servicer and say plainly: "I can't afford this payment — what are my options?" That sentence starts the process. Servicers deal with it constantly; there's no shame in it, and asking early keeps you out of default.

forgiveness, honestly

Forgiveness is real but narrower than headlines suggest, and it applies to federal loans only.

Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF) can wipe out the remaining balance after 120 qualifying monthly payments (about 10 years) while working full-time for a government or eligible nonprofit employer. It's genuinely powerful for teachers, nurses, public defenders, and similar roles — but the eligibility rules are specific, so track your qualifying payments and certify your employment regularly through the PSLF tool rather than assuming you're on track.

IDR forgiveness forgives whatever remains at the end of an income-driven plan's term. It takes a long time and the rules are in flux, but for borrowers with high balances relative to income, it's a real backstop.

Given how much is changing, the honest move is to make forgiveness a bonus you plan around, not the entire plan — and to confirm the current rules at the source every year.

questions people actually ask

should i pay off student loans or invest?

Compare the rates. If your loan is around 6–7% and you have high-interest credit card debt at 22%, the card wins first. Against investing, it's closer: a 6.4% guaranteed 'return' from paying down the loan is solid, though many people do both — capture any employer 401(k) match first (it's free money), then split between the loan and investing. See where to actually put your money for the order.

is refinancing my student loans a good idea?

For private loans, refinancing to a lower rate is often a clear win. For federal loans, refinancing means moving them to a private lender — which permanently forfeits income-driven plans, forgiveness, and hardship protections. Only consider it if you have stable high income, no interest in forgiveness, and plan to pay off quickly. When unsure, keep federal loans federal.

what actually happens if i ignore them?

For federal loans: after enough missed payments you enter default, which can mean a damaged credit score, wage garnishment, and seized tax refunds — while the balance keeps growing. The important part is that every one of those outcomes is avoidable with an income-driven plan or a forbearance, which is why calling your servicer early matters so much.

how do i even find my loans and servicer?

Log in to StudentAid.gov for all federal loans and your assigned servicer, listed under 'My Aid.' For private loans, check your credit report (free at annualcreditreport.com) or old paperwork. Knowing exactly what you owe, to whom, and at what rate is step one of any plan — and it's the step anxiety most often blocks.
