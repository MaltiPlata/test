start an etsy shop (with the real numbers)

you can open a shop this afternoon for about $10. whether it makes money depends on things nobody puts in the tutorial — so here are the fees, the margins, and the earnings reality first.

8 min readupdated august 2026topic: build

the short version

  • opening a shop is nearly free: $0.20 per listing, no monthly subscription required.
  • etsy takes roughly 9.5–10.5% of each sale in standard fees — 6.5% transaction + ~3% + $0.25 processing.
  • the ambush: offsite ads are mandatory once you pass $10,000/year, and take another 12–15% of any sale they drive.
  • the honest number: the median etsy seller earns under $500 a year. about 5% treat it as primary income.
  • digital products carry 70–90% margins versus 20–40% on handmade — and can be started for under $50.

what it actually costs to open

The barrier is genuinely low, which is both the appeal and the problem — it's low for everyone, so competition is intense.

To open: a $0.20 listing fee per item and no required monthly subscription. Some sellers report a one-time setup fee between $15 and $29, and new sellers can often find a referral link giving the first 40 listings free — worth hunting for before you pay anything.

Realistic startup costs beyond the platform, though: materials $50–$500+, photography $50–$200, initial inventory $100–$1,000+, packaging $50–$200 — while digital sellers can start for under $50.

the fee stack, in order

Etsy's fees aren't one number, they're a stack — and the surprise is always the same one.

feeamountwhen
listing$0.20per item listed, and again per unit sold
transaction6.5%on item + shipping + gift wrap
payment processing~3% + $0.25US sellers; varies by country
offsite ads12–15%on ad-driven sales — mandatory above $10k/yr
currency conversion~2.5%international sales

Standard fees land at roughly 9.5–10.5% of each sale for a US seller. On a $20 item with $5 shipping, that's about $2.43 in total fees.

the fee that ambushes successful sellers

Offsite Ads. Etsy advertises your listings on Google, Meta and elsewhere — and once your shop passes $10,000 in annual revenue you cannot opt out. Etsy then takes 12–15% of any sale attributed to those ads, on top of everything else, which can push a single sale to 22–25% of the sale price in total fees before your cost of goods. Price for this before you scale, not after.

Two smaller things worth knowing: the transaction fee applies to shipping you charge the buyer, not just the item, and multi-quantity orders incur a $0.20 listing fee per unit sold, not per order.

One way to claw fees back: Etsy's Share & Save program drops your transaction fee from 6.5% to 2.5% on sales you drive yourself through a trackable link. It's free to join, and it rewards exactly the thing you should be doing anyway — bringing your own audience.

the earnings reality, before you quit anything

Most Etsy content is written by people selling you courses or print-on-demand subscriptions. Here are the numbers they skip.

The median Etsy seller earns under $500 a year. The top 10% earn $1,000–$5,000 a month — but typically after two to three years of building. About 5% treat it as primary income.

That is not a reason to skip it. It's a reason to keep your expectations calibrated and your day job for now. Most people who do well either sell digital products at scale, hold a niche with high margins and steady demand, or run Etsy alongside their own website.

the margin difference is the whole game

Handmade physical goods run 20–40% profit margins; digital downloads run 70–90%. A digital product — a planner, a template, wall art, a spreadsheet — is made once and sold infinitely, with no materials, no shipping, and no inventory. If you're starting from zero with limited cash, that asymmetry should probably decide what you sell.

On time: product creation ranges from about an hour for a digital download to 10+ hours for complex handmade items, plus 2–5 hours weekly for photography, listings, customer service and shipping. First sale typically lands one to three months in, with decent photos and SEO.

the setup, step by step

  1. Decide digital or physical first. This determines your costs, your margins, and how much of your life the shop consumes. Be honest about which one you'd still be doing in six months.
  2. Price backwards from the fees. Start with what you want to clear, add your cost of goods, then add roughly 10% for standard fees — and if you plan to grow past $10k, model 22–25% so offsite ads don't erase your margin later.
  3. Open the shop. Name, banner, policies, and payment setup. Hunt for a free-listings referral link before you list.
  4. Photograph properly. This is the single highest-leverage thing on a visual marketplace. Natural light and a clean background beat expensive gear.
  5. Write listings for search. Etsy is a search engine. Titles and tags should use the words a buyer would type, not clever product names.
  6. Handle the money side from day one. Separate account, track every fee and material cost, and set aside for taxes — Etsy income is self-employment income.
  7. Drive your own traffic. Share & Save pays you for it, and it's the only part of your visibility that Etsy's algorithm can't take away.

the tax part nobody mentions in the tutorial

Etsy profit is self-employment income. Once your net profit passes $400 you owe self-employment tax, whether or not a form arrives — and a common rule of thumb is setting aside 25–30% as you earn. Track material costs all year; they're deductible and they're impossible to reconstruct in April.

the risk worth naming

You are building on rented land. Etsy controls your visibility, changes its algorithm, sets its fees, and raised its transaction fee from 5% to 6.5% in 2022 without asking anyone.

That's a reasonable trade at the start — you're renting access to a huge built-in audience instead of building one from scratch. But it means the most valuable asset you can build is the part Etsy doesn't own: an email list, a following, or eventually your own storefront.

The sellers who last generally treat Etsy as a customer-acquisition channel rather than the whole business.

the bottom line

Etsy is one of the lowest-risk ways to test whether people will pay for something you make. Ten dollars and an afternoon buys you a real answer, which is cheaper than almost any other form of market research.

Just go in with the actual numbers: about 10% in fees before ads, 22–25% after, a median seller earning under $500 a year, and digital products carrying two to three times the margin of handmade.

Your next tiny step: price one product backwards. Take what you'd want to clear, add your materials, then add 25%. If the resulting price is one a stranger would actually pay, you have something. If not, better to learn that now than after buying inventory.

questions people actually ask

how much does etsy take per sale?

Roughly 9.5–10.5% for a US seller in standard fees: a 6.5% transaction fee, about 3% + $0.25 payment processing, plus $0.20 per listing sold.

If the sale comes through Offsite Ads, add another 12–15%, bringing the total to 22–25% before your cost of goods.

can i really start with no money?

Close to it for digital products — under $50 is realistic, and listings are $0.20 each.

Physical products need materials, packaging and often inventory, which realistically runs a few hundred dollars before your first sale.

do i need an llc to sell on etsy?

No. You can sell as a sole proprietor, and most new sellers do.

An LLC becomes worth considering when you have real revenue, real liability exposure, or personal assets to protect — see our LLC walkthrough for what it actually costs.

what sells best?

Digital downloads have the best margins. Among physical goods, personalized and custom items, wedding-related products, and home decor tend to perform well.

Competition is intense in every popular category, which is why a specific niche usually beats a broad one.

when do i owe taxes on this?

Etsy profit is self-employment income. Once net profit passes $400 you generally owe self-employment tax regardless of whether you receive a 1099.

Set aside roughly 25–30% of profit as you earn, and track material costs all year — they reduce what you owe.
