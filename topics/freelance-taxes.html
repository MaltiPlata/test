1099 & freelance taxes

When you have a job, your employer quietly takes taxes out before you ever see the money. When you work for yourself, nobody does that. This is the bill people don't see coming — and exactly how to make sure it never surprises you.

11 min readupdated august 2026topic: taxes

the short version

  • Freelance income is taxed twice over: regular income tax, plus 15.3% self-employment tax that a job would normally split with you.
  • You owe self-employment tax once your net profit hits $400. That threshold hasn't moved since 1990.
  • Not getting a 1099 doesn't mean you don't owe. In 2026 the threshold jumped from $600 to $2,000 — so far fewer forms will arrive, and the tax is owed either way.
  • If you'll owe $1,000 or more, the IRS wants payments four times a year, not once.
  • The safety net is safe harbor: pay 100% of last year's total tax (110% if you earned over $150k) and you can't be penalized, no matter what you actually end up owing.
  • Rough rule: set aside 25–30% of every payment the moment it lands. In a separate account.

why the bill is bigger than you expect

Here's what an employer does that you may never have noticed. Social Security and Medicare taxes total 15.3% of your pay. At a job, your employer pays half of that and you pay the other half, which shows up on your pay stub as FICA.

When you work for yourself, you are both the employer and the employee. So you pay both halves. That's self-employment tax, and it's the single biggest reason freelancers get blindsided.

Self-employment tax is 15.3% of 92.35% of your net profit — on $5,000 of profit, that's roughly $707. And that's before any regular income tax.

the actual surprise

Someone leaves a $60,000 job to freelance and bills $60,000 in her first year. It feels like the same money. It isn't — she now owes roughly $4,600 more in self-employment tax alone than she did as an employee, and nothing was withheld along the way. In April, that arrives as one number.

One partial consolation: half of your self-employment tax is deductible on Schedule 1 — it doesn't reduce the SE tax itself, but it does lower your overall bill.

the 1099, and the trap inside it

A 1099-NEC is the form a client sends when they've paid you as a contractor. It's the freelance equivalent of a W-2 — except a W-2 comes with taxes already taken out, and a 1099 comes with nothing taken out at all.

2026 changed the rules, and not in the direction you'd assume. The reporting threshold for Form 1099-NEC and 1099-MISC rose from $600 to $2,000 for payments made in 2026, with inflation adjustments starting in 2027. The 1099-K threshold — the one for PayPal, Venmo, Stripe, and Etsy — reverted to $20,000 and more than 200 transactions.

read this part twice

Raising the threshold only changes who receives a form — not who owes tax. All self-employment income is taxable from the first dollar, and self-employment tax applies to net earnings over $400.

So 2026 is the year fewer forms show up and the obligation stays exactly the same. If you billed six clients $1,500 each, you may receive zero 1099s and still owe tax on $9,000. The forms were never the thing that made it taxable.

Which means your own records are now the source of truth, not the paperwork that arrives in your mailbox. If you take one habit from this page, take that one.

the four dates

You must make quarterly estimated payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more in federal tax for the year after withholding and refundable credits — a threshold most freelancers cross within a few months of work.

The 2026 dates are April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15, 2027.

They're not evenly spaced, which catches people every year — there are only two months between the April and June payments. Put all four in your calendar now, with a reminder two weeks ahead of each.

the safe harbor, which is the part that actually protects you

You don't have to predict your income correctly. There's a rule that makes penalties impossible if you follow it.

Pay at least 100% of your prior-year total tax — 110% if your AGI exceeded $150,000 — split across the four payments, and you won't owe a penalty regardless of how much you actually end up owing.

Take last year's total tax, divide by four, pay that. You may still owe a balance in April if you had a great year, but you cannot be penalized. For anyone whose income swings unpredictably, this is the whole strategy.

if you also have a W-2 job

There's an easier route. Instead of mailing quarterly vouchers, increase the withholding at your job — the IRS treats withholding as paid evenly across all quarters, even if you change it in November.

what you can subtract before any of this applies

Self-employment tax applies to your net profit — what's left after legitimate business expenses. Every real expense you fail to record is money you're taxed on unnecessarily.

The ones people most often miss:

  • Mileage. The 2026 business mileage rate is 72.5 cents through June 30 and 76 cents from July 1 — so you'll need to split your log by date this year.
  • The home office, if a space is used regularly and exclusively for work.
  • Health insurance premiums, if you're self-employed and not eligible for a spouse's plan.
  • A retirement account. A SEP-IRA or solo 401(k) reduces taxable income substantially — the options are here.
  • Software, phone, internet, professional fees — the business-use share.

There's also the QBI deduction, a break specifically for self-employed people and small business owners. It was made permanent and sits at 23% for 2026. Your tax software or preparer should apply it automatically, but it's worth knowing it exists.

the system, in five steps

  1. Open a second checking account the day you start freelancing. Call it "taxes." This is the single highest-leverage thing on this page.
  2. Move 25–30% of every payment into it the moment the money lands. Not monthly. Not at quarter-end. Immediately, while it still feels like the client's money rather than yours.
  3. Track income and expenses somewhere real — a spreadsheet is completely fine. Since fewer 1099s will arrive in 2026, your records are the record.
  4. Pay quarterly from that account on the four dates, using the safe harbor number if your income is unpredictable. Pay directly at IRS Direct Pay; it's free and instant.
  5. Check your state. Most states want their own estimated payments, on their own schedule, with their own thresholds.

Step 2 is the whole thing. Nearly every freelance tax disaster is the same story: the money arrived, it got spent, and the bill came later for money that no longer existed.

the bottom line

Freelance taxes aren't harder than employee taxes. They're just visible. The same money was always leaving your paycheck — you simply never had to look at it, because someone else handled it before you saw the balance.

Set aside 25–30% from the first payment, put four dates in your calendar, and this becomes an admin task instead of an emergency.

questions people actually ask

i only made a few thousand dollars. do i really have to do this?

If your net profit from self-employment was $400 or more, you have to file Schedule SE and pay self-employment tax. That threshold is genuinely that low, and it hasn't changed since 1990.

Quarterly payments are a separate question — those kick in when you expect to owe $1,000 or more in total federal tax.

i never got a 1099. does that mean it's not taxable?

No. This is the most expensive misunderstanding in freelancing. The 1099 is a reporting form, not the thing that creates the tax. Income is taxable whether or not a form documents it.

2026 makes this more important, not less — with the threshold at $2,000, far fewer forms will be issued for the same income.

what happens if i just skip the quarterly payments?

You'd owe an underpayment penalty plus interest on top of the tax. It isn't catastrophic on small amounts, but it's an avoidable cost — and the bigger risk is arriving at April with a bill you haven't set money aside for.

If you've already missed a quarter, pay as soon as you can rather than waiting for the next date. The penalty accrues by time.

should i form an LLC to save on taxes?

A single-member LLC by itself changes nothing about your taxes — you still file Schedule C and still pay self-employment tax. What it provides is liability separation, which is a different benefit and often a good one.

The structure that can reduce self-employment tax is an S-corp election, and it generally only makes sense above a certain profit level because it adds payroll requirements and cost. That's a conversation for a CPA with your actual numbers. What an LLC does and doesn't do →

do i need an accountant?

For a straightforward freelance year, good tax software handles it. Consider a CPA when you have employees or contractors, income in multiple states, an S-corp question, or a year strange enough that you're guessing.

One session with a CPA in your first year is often worth it purely to confirm your setup is right.
