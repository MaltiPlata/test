when money is the leash

controlling the money is one of the most common ways one partner controls another — and one of the least recognized. here's what it looks like, and what you can do quietly, at your own pace.

9 min readupdated august 2026topic: safety

the short version

  • financial abuse appears in 99% of domestic violence cases — and it's cited as the number one reason people stay in or return to abusive relationships.
  • 78% of Americans don't recognize it as abuse, which is exactly why it works so well and why it takes so long to name.
  • it looks like: controlling all the accounts, an "allowance," debt taken out in your name, sabotaging your job, or making you account for every dollar.
  • it doesn't stop at separation — economic abuse doesn't require physical proximity, and often continues after leaving.
  • the money steps below are meant to be done quietly and in whatever order is safe for you. safety comes before optimization, always.

what it actually is

Financial abuse is behavior that controls someone's ability to get, use, or keep money — with the effect, and usually the intent, of making leaving impossible.

It's not a fringe pattern. Research from the Center for Financial Security finds financial abuse occurs in 99% of domestic violence cases, and in 2021 the Violence Against Women Act was updated to formally name economic abuse as “behavior that is coercive, deceptive, or unreasonably controls or restrains a person's ability to acquire, use, or maintain economic resources”.

And yet 78% of Americans don't recognize financial abuse as a form of domestic violence. That gap is the whole problem. It means people living inside it often don't have a word for what's happening — only a persistent sense that something is wrong and a belief that they're bad with money.

why this is the reason people stay

Economic abuse is cited as the main reason victims stay in or return to abusive relationships. Not lack of will. Not confusion about whether it's bad. Money. If you have no account, no credit in your name, and no way to make first and last month's rent, the exit isn't a decision — it's a logistics problem. Recognizing that reframes the question from "why didn't she leave" to "what would she have needed."

what it looks like from the inside

It rarely arrives labeled. It often starts as care — let me handle this, you have enough going on — and narrows over time.

Common patterns, drawn from NNEDV and PCADV:

  • Controlling access. All accounts in their name, or joint accounts you can't actually reach. Passwords you don't have.
  • The allowance. Being given money for approved expenses and having to justify or produce receipts for each one.
  • Coerced debt. Credit cards or loans opened in your name, sometimes without your knowledge. This one follows you for years.
  • Employment sabotage. Making you late, forcing you to miss work, harassing you at work, or forbidding job training and advancement opportunities. Sabotaging the car so you can't get there.
  • Hiding the picture. Secret income, hidden accounts, refusing to share tax returns or what's actually owed.
  • Benefits control. Taking public benefits, or threatening to report you for "misusing" them.

The costs are measurable and long. Survivors lose roughly $53,000 in wages over a lifetime and 8 million days of paid work a year collectively — and it happens across every socioeconomic, educational, racial, and ethnic group. Income and education don't protect anyone from this.

it doesn't end when you leave

This is the part almost nothing prepares people for. Economic abuse doesn't rely on physical proximity, so it can continue after separation — and those who leave are often left in debt or without the financial footing to rebuild.

Post-separation forms include coerced debt still sitting on your credit report, refusal to pay court-ordered support, deliberately dragging out legal proceedings to drain your money, and using custody filings as a way to keep contact and control. Some advocates call this legal abuse, and courts are only beginning to recognize the pattern.

Knowing this in advance isn't discouraging — it's the difference between being blindsided and being prepared. It's also why the credit and documentation steps below matter as much as the immediate ones.

money steps, at your own pace

Safety first, always. Some of these can escalate risk if discovered. A local advocate can help you sequence them for your specific situation — that's exactly what the hotline is for, and the call is free and confidential.

  1. Gather documents where they can't be destroyed. Birth certificate, ID, passport, Social Security card, bank and insurance details, tax returns. Keep them with a trusted person or in a safety deposit box. Photos on a private cloud account they don't know about work too.
  2. Know the full picture before you need it. Account numbers, balances, debts, income, where the tax returns are. Information is portable and invisible.
  3. Pull your own credit reports. Free weekly at annualcreditreport.com. This is how you find debt opened in your name. Do it from a device they don't monitor.
  4. Open an account they don't know about, if it's safe. Different bank, statements paperless or to a trusted address. Even small, regular amounts add up to a first month's rent.
  5. Build credit in your own name. One card, small recurring charge, paid in full. This is the slowest step, which is why it's worth starting earliest.
  6. Set up an email and phone they don't have access to. Everything financial routes through these.
  7. Talk to an advocate before big moves. Local DV programs often have economic-justice staff who help with coerced debt, benefits, and housing — free.

on coerced debt

Debt taken out in your name under coercion is a recognized problem, and there are sometimes routes to dispute or discharge it — identity-theft procedures, state-specific protections, and advocates who specialize in it. It's genuinely difficult, and it is not hopeless. Don't assume you're stuck with it without asking someone who does this work.

if you're worried about someone

The most useful thing you can do is usually the least dramatic: stay connected, don't make it conditional, and don't push a timeline.

What tends to help: offering to hold documents or mail, offering a small amount of untracked cash if you can, letting them use your address or device, and saying plainly that you'll help whenever they're ready and won't be offended if that's not now.

What tends to backfire: ultimatums, criticizing their partner in a way that makes them defend the relationship, or making support contingent on leaving immediately. Isolation is the mechanism — being one person who doesn't disappear is genuinely protective.

the bottom line

If any of this described your relationship, the important thing to take from it is this: the difficulty getting to money is not evidence that you're bad with money. It's a documented pattern with a name, present in nearly every abusive relationship, and it is designed to feel like your own failure.

You don't have to decide anything today. Information and preparation are not the same as leaving, and they're yours to hold quietly for as long as you need.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline is free, confidential, and 24/7: 1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788, or chat at thehotline.org. They can connect you to local advocates who handle exactly the money pieces above.

questions people actually ask

is it still abuse if they've never been physical?

Yes. Domestic violence is a pattern of coercive, controlling behavior that can be physical, emotional, sexual, technological, or financial — and financial abuse frequently occurs with no physical violence at all.

The updated Violence Against Women Act specifically names economic abuse as a form of coercive control.

what if we're just bad at talking about money?

Ordinary money conflict is mutual — you argue, you're both frustrated, you both have access to information.

Abuse has a direction: one person controls access and the other has to account for themselves. If you're asking permission, hiding purchases out of fear, or can't see the accounts, that's a different thing than disagreeing about budgets.

can i get out of debt they put in my name?

Sometimes. Coerced debt is increasingly recognized, and there are routes through identity-theft procedures, state protections, and advocates who specialize in it.

It's often difficult and it's frequently worth attempting. Start with a local DV program's economic-justice staff rather than a for-profit credit-repair company.

i already left and the money mess followed me. is that normal?

Unfortunately yes. Economic abuse doesn't require proximity and commonly continues after separation — through coerced debt, withheld support, and drawn-out legal proceedings.

It's a known pattern, not a sign you did the exit wrong.

how do i browse safely?

Use a device the person doesn't have access to when possible — a library computer, a friend's phone, or a work device if that's safe.

Private/incognito windows help but aren't foolproof if monitoring software is installed. When in doubt, a different device is safer than a different browser mode.
