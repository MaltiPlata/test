retirement · caregiving

the spousal ira

you don't have a paycheck, so you assume you can't have a retirement account. that assumption is wrong, and it's costing you the most valuable years you'll ever have — the early ones.

8 min readupdated august 2026topic: retirement

the short version

  • normally you need earned income to fund an IRA. the spousal IRA rule is the exception: your spouse's income counts as yours for this purpose.
  • in 2026 you can put in $7,500 — or $8,600 if you're 50 or older. a one-earner couple can fund two IRAs, $15,000 total.
  • three conditions, that's all: you're married, you file jointly, and your spouse earned at least as much as you're both contributing.
  • it is not a joint account. it's yours — your name, your social security number, your beneficiaries, your investment choices.
  • there's no such thing as a "spousal IRA" product. you open a normal traditional or Roth IRA. the rule is invisible; it lives on your tax return.

the rule nobody mentions

Here's the default rule for retirement accounts: to put money in an IRA, you need earned income — wages, salary, tips, bonuses, commissions, or self-employment income. No paycheck, no contribution. Which is why a lot of people who left work to raise kids or care for a parent quietly conclude that retirement saving is on hold until they go back.

The exception has a name almost nobody says out loud: the Kay Bailey Hutchison Spousal IRA provision, which lets a non-working spouse contribute to their own IRA based on their partner's income, as long as you file a joint return.

Read that again, because it's the whole article: your spouse's income makes you eligible, and the account is still entirely yours.

the part that matters most

An IRA can only be owned by one person — there is no such thing as a joint IRA. Even though eligibility comes from the working spouse's compensation, the account is owned entirely by the non-working spouse. Your name. Your SSN. Your beneficiaries. You pick what it's invested in.

what you can actually put in, in 2026

The IRS raised the limits this year. The 2026 IRA contribution limit is $7,500, and the catch-up for anyone 50 or older is $1,100, bringing that total to $8,600.

what a one-earner couple can fund across two IRAs

That last number is the one people miss. The limit applies per IRA, not per household — so a couple with one working spouse can put $7,500 into each of two accounts, $15,000 combined, as long as the working spouse earned at least $15,000. Your household's tax-advantaged capacity roughly doubles the moment you open the second account.

One ceiling to know: you can't contribute more than 100% of the taxable compensation on the return. If your spouse earned $9,000 last year, $9,000 is the cap across both accounts, not $15,000.

the three conditions

  1. You're married. That's it — there's no requirement that you ever worked, and the SECURE Act removed the old age limit on contributions.
  2. You file a joint return. Married filing separately cannot use the spousal IRA rule at all — this is the requirement that trips people up most often.
  3. Your spouse's earned income covers both contributions. Wages, salary, tips, bonuses, commissions, self-employment. Investment income, rental income, pensions, and Social Security don't count.

If you contribute assuming you'll file jointly and then file separately, your contributions become excess contributions, and you have to remove them plus earnings by the filing deadline to avoid a 6% penalty. Worth knowing before a chaotic tax year.

traditional or Roth?

You're choosing when you pay tax. Traditional: deduct now, pay tax on withdrawals later. Roth: no deduction now, withdrawals are tax-free later. You can split between the two, but the combined total across all your IRAs still can't exceed $7,500.

Those spousal figures come straight from the IRS: for a contributor not covered by a workplace plan who is married to someone who is, the 2026 phase-out runs from $242,000 to $252,000. That's a far more generous range than the $129,000–$149,000 that applies to the covered spouse — which is exactly why the non-working spouse's IRA is often the better-treated account in the household.

For most single-income families the income question is moot, and the real decision is simpler: if you expect to be in a low tax bracket now and a higher one later, Roth tends to win. Caregiving years are usually low-income years, which is precisely when Roth is most attractive. How to actually open and fund one covers the mechanics.

why "his account is our account" isn't enough

The common objection: we're married, his 401(k) is our money, why does a second account matter?

Three reasons, and none of them require assuming a marriage will fail.

Ownership is not the same as a claim. Retirement assets are generally part of a divorce settlement regardless of whose name is on them — but a claim you'd have to assert through a legal process is different from an account you already own. One requires a lawyer and a court order. The other requires a login.

Two accounts create flexibility a single account can't. Two sets of beneficiary designations, more room for Roth conversions later, and more control over which dollars get tapped first in retirement.

Women spend more years in retirement. That's the whole argument of the longevity gap — more years to fund, and historically a smaller pot to fund them from.

the honest framing

Caregiving isn't a break from contributing to the household — it's unpaid work that lets the other income exist. The spousal IRA is the one place the tax code more or less acknowledges that. Using it isn't hedging against your marriage. It's making the household's books reflect what's actually happening in it.

how to actually do it

  1. Pick a brokerage. Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all offer IRAs with no account fee and no minimum to open. You do not need a "spousal IRA" product — there isn't one.
  2. Open it in your name. Your name, your SSN. If the application asks about employment, "not employed" is a normal answer and doesn't disqualify you.
  3. Choose traditional or Roth using the table above.
  4. Move the money in. From the joint checking account is completely fine — the source of the cash doesn't matter, only that the return shows enough earned income.
  5. Actually invest it. Money that lands in an IRA sits in cash until you buy something. A low-cost target-date fund or a total-market index fund is the standard boring answer.
  6. Set it to repeat. $625 a month is the 2026 max. Any amount counts; the automation is what makes it happen.

Step 5 is where most first-timers stall out. An IRA is a container, not an investment — funding it and leaving it uninvested is the single most common mistake.

One deadline worth knowing: 2026 contributions can be made until April 15, 2027, but contributions made after January 1, 2027 must be specifically designated as 2026 contributions with your custodian. Miss the label and it counts for the wrong year.

the bottom line

If you're married, filing jointly, and your spouse works, you can have $7,500 a year in a retirement account with your name on it, starting this year. That's true whether you've been out of the workforce for eight months or eighteen years.

The reason this matters more than the number suggests is time. A contribution made during the caregiving years has decades to compound — and those are exactly the years most likely to get skipped.

questions people actually ask

do i need to have ever had a job?

No. There's no work-history requirement at all. The only income that matters is your spouse's, and only for establishing that the household earned at least as much as you're contributing.

is it a joint account with my spouse?

No — and this is the most common misunderstanding. IRAs are always individually owned. It's your account, under your SSN, and you name the beneficiaries.

The "spousal" part describes only how you became eligible.

what if i work part-time?

Then you can contribute up to your own earned income, and use the spousal rule to cover the rest up to the annual limit. Earning $3,000 doesn't cap you at $3,000 if your spouse's income covers the difference.

does social security or child support count as income for this?

No. Only earned income counts — wages, salary, tips, bonuses, commissions, and self-employment income. Social Security, investment income, rental income, and pensions do not.

can i still do this if we're separated but not divorced?

Only if you still file a joint return for that tax year. Married filing separately makes you ineligible for the spousal rule. This is worth raising with a tax professional, because filing status during a separation has effects well beyond the IRA.

what if my spouse doesn't want to do this?

It's a household budgeting conversation, not a legal one — the money has to come from somewhere. Framing it as doubling the household's tax-advantaged space, rather than as a personal account, tends to land better, because that's genuinely what it does.
