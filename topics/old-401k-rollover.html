the 401(k) you left at an old job

there are about 32 million abandoned 401(k)s in america holding $2.1 trillion. if you've changed jobs, one of them might be yours — and finding it is an afternoon's work.

7 min readupdated august 2026topic: retirement

the short version

  • there are roughly 31.9 million forgotten 401(k) accounts holding about $2.1 trillion — averaging around $66,000 each.
  • you have four options: leave it, roll it to your new employer's plan, roll it to an IRA, or cash out.
  • cashing out is the expensive one — income tax plus a 10% penalty under 59½ — and yet an estimated 41% of people do it when they leave a job.
  • leaving it isn't free either: dormant accounts can carry admin fees and sit in default investments that no longer fit you.
  • if you do an indirect rollover, you have 60 days to redeposit or it becomes a taxable distribution. direct transfers avoid this entirely.

how common this is

If you've forgotten a retirement account, you are not disorganized — you're typical. Capitalize estimates roughly 31.9 million forgotten 401(k) accounts holding about $2.1 trillion, averaging about $66,000 per account.

The cause is structural: a labor market built for mobility meeting a retirement system built around single-employer plans. Every job change is a fork where an account gets rolled forward or quietly left behind, and the default is behind.

Note that "leave it" is only available above a threshold — balances between $1,000 and $7,000 can be rolled into an IRA without your approval, and under $1,000 can be automatically cashed out and mailed to you as a taxable distribution. So ignoring a small balance doesn't mean it stays put.

why cashing out costs so much more than it looks

An estimated 41.4% of people cash out their 401(k) when they leave an employer. You lose income tax on the full amount, plus a 10% early-withdrawal penalty if you're under 59½, plus — the biggest one — every future dollar that money would have compounded into. A $20,000 balance isn't $20,000; at typical long-run growth it's a meaningful multiple of that by retirement.

why 'just leave it' isn't free

Leaving it alone feels neutral. It usually isn't.

Some plans charge former employees administrative fees — PensionBee estimates a seemingly small $4.55 monthly fee can drain more than $1,600 from an account over 30 years. More significantly, dormant accounts often sit in default allocations that no longer match your age or goals, and nobody is rebalancing them.

Capitalize notes an additional wrinkle: under SECURE 2.0, more accounts are subject to forced rollovers into “Safe Harbor IRAs” that typically invest in conservative, cash-like vehicles with returns falling short of a diversified portfolio. Your retirement money can end up parked in something close to cash for a decade without you being told.

There's also an administrative trap later: required minimum distributions are calculated across your accounts, and a forgotten pot of money can throw off that calculation and trigger a penalty.

how to find one you've lost

  1. List every employer where you contributed. Even briefly. Old W-2s and tax returns will jog your memory, and pay stubs show retirement deductions.
  2. Check your old email. Search for the plan provider names — the big recordkeepers send annual statements, and the account may still be tied to a personal email.
  3. Contact the employer's HR or benefits line. They can tell you which recordkeeper held the plan, even if the company has since merged.
  4. Search the federal databases. The Department of Labor's abandoned-plan database and the PBGC's unclaimed-pension search both exist for exactly this. Your state's unclaimed-property office is also worth a search — old accounts sometimes escheat there.
  5. Update your contact info once found. Old addresses are the main reason these go dark in the first place.

how to roll it over without a tax bill

There are two mechanics, and the difference matters enormously.

Direct rollover (also called trustee-to-trustee): the money moves from the old plan to the new account without passing through your hands. No withholding, no deadline, no risk. This is the one to ask for by name.

Indirect rollover: they send you a check. Now you have 60 days to deposit the money into a new eligible retirement account or face taxes and penalties — and the plan typically withholds 20% up front, which you must replace from your own pocket to roll the full amount, then reclaim at tax time.

the sentence to say

Call the old plan's recordkeeper and say: "I'd like to do a direct rollover of my full balance to [new provider], made payable to the receiving institution, not to me." That one phrasing avoids the withholding, the deadline, and the most common expensive mistake in this entire process.

One more choice to make deliberately: if your old plan holds pre-tax dollars and you roll into a Roth IRA, that's a conversion and it's taxable this year. Rolling pre-tax into a traditional IRA is not. Match the tax treatment unless you're deliberately converting.

the bottom line

This is money you already earned, already saved, and already own. The only thing standing between you and it is usually a phone call and an old address.

And the average forgotten account is around $66,000 — an afternoon of admin for a number most people would never leave on a table if they could see it.

Your next tiny step: write down every employer you ever contributed to a 401(k) with. Just the list. That's the whole first move, and it takes five minutes.

questions people actually ask

can i roll a 401(k) into an ira myself?

Yes, and it's usually straightforward. Open the IRA first, then ask the old plan for a direct rollover made payable to the receiving institution.

Most brokerages have a rollover team that will handle the call with you, because they want the assets.

what if the company i worked for doesn't exist anymore?

The plan assets still exist. Try the Department of Labor's abandoned plan database, the PBGC's unclaimed pension search, and your state's unclaimed-property office.

Mergers and closures don't erase your account — they just make it harder to find.

should i roll into my new 401(k) or an ira?

An IRA usually gives you far more investment choice and often lower fees. A workplace plan preserves access to plan loans and generally stronger creditor protection.

If your new plan has good low-cost funds and you like having one account, consolidating there is reasonable too.

i cashed one out years ago. how bad was it?

You paid income tax and likely a 10% penalty, and you lost the future compounding. It's done, and it's genuinely common — over 40% of people do it.

The useful move now is making sure it doesn't happen with the next one.

is there a deadline to roll over?

Not for a direct rollover — you can do it years later. The 60-day deadline only applies to indirect rollovers where a check is issued to you.

Which is the main reason to always ask for a direct rollover.
