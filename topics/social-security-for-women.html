the grown-up stuff · retirement

social security, the part nobody tells you

there's a rule that could be worth six figures to you, and most people who qualify have never heard of it. here's how spousal, divorced-spouse, and survivor benefits actually work.

9 min readupdated august 2026topic: retirement

the short version

  • if your marriage lasted at least 10 years and you're currently unmarried and 62+, you can claim on your ex-spouse's record — worth up to 50% of their full benefit.
  • your ex doesn't need to know, doesn't have to agree, and their check isn't reduced. their new spouse can claim at the same time as you.
  • if your ex has died, survivor benefits can be worth up to 100% of their benefit — and can start as early as 60.
  • the 10-year rule is brutally literal. nine years and 364 days does not qualify. if you're near the line and divorcing, the finalization date matters.
  • you don't get both — you get the larger of your own benefit or the spousal one.

why this matters more for women

Social Security was built around a mid-century assumption: one earner, one homemaker, one marriage that lasts. Real life stopped looking like that decades ago, but the rules that protect people whose lives didn't follow that script are still on the books — just badly publicized.

The gap they're meant to soften is measurable. In 2022, the average monthly Social Security check for women 65 and older was $1,638, versus $2,020 for men — roughly 20% less. That's not because women claimed wrong. It's because benefits are calculated from lifetime earnings, and lifetime earnings absorb every year of lower pay and every year spent caregiving.

Which makes the rules below unusually valuable: they're one of the few places where the system quietly gives some of that back — if you know to ask.

the three benefits, quickly

Most people know about the first one and are fuzzy on the rest.

  • Your own retirement benefit — based on your work record. You need 40 credits to qualify; in 2026 one credit is $1,890, and you can earn up to four a year, so roughly ten years of work.
  • A spousal benefit — worth up to half of what your spouse qualifies for at their full retirement age.
  • A survivor benefit — paid when a spouse (or qualifying ex-spouse) dies, worth up to 100% of their benefit.

Full retirement age (FRA) is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later. Claiming earlier permanently reduces the monthly amount.

the divorced-spouse rule

This is the one. If you were married for at least ten years and then divorced, you may be able to claim a benefit on your ex-spouse's earnings record.

The conditions, per SSA rules:

  1. The marriage lasted 10 years or more, measured from the wedding date to the date the divorce was finalized. Periods of separation inside the marriage don't count against you.
  2. You are currently unmarried. If you remarried and that marriage ended, you may regain eligibility on the first ex's record.
  3. You're at least 62.
  4. The benefit would be larger than your own retirement benefit. You receive the higher of the two, not both.

the three things that shock people

Your ex doesn't have to know. There's no notification, no permission, no conversation. It doesn't reduce their check by a dollar — and if they've remarried, their new spouse can claim on the same record at the same time you do. And they don't even have to have filed yet: a divorced spouse can claim once both people are 62 and the divorce has been final for at least two continuous years, even if your ex is still working and hasn't touched their own benefit.

Read that again if you were married a decade to someone who out-earned you and then divorced. This is money the system already set aside for the years you were part of that household.

the ten-year line is not a suggestion

The rule is exact and unforgiving. If your marriage lasted nine years and 364 days, you don't qualify — there's no partial credit, no rounding, no appeal to fairness.

Which produces an uncomfortable but genuinely important piece of information: if you're approaching a divorce and the marriage is close to ten years, the finalization date is a financial decision, not just a legal one. Nobody wants to hear that. It has also been worth a great deal of money to people who heard it in time.

two exceptions worth knowing

The 10-year requirement doesn't apply if you're caring for your former spouse's child who is under 16 or has a qualifying disability, and the child qualifies on that record. Separately, if you divorced and then remarried the same person, the intervening divorce can be disregarded and the marriage treated as continuous for the 10-year count. Both are narrow — confirm with SSA rather than assuming.

survivor benefits — bigger, and different rules

If your ex-spouse dies, the math changes substantially in your favor. A divorced-spouse benefit on a living ex maxes out around 50%. A divorced survivor benefit can be worth up to 100% of the deceased ex-spouse's full benefit.

You can claim survivor benefits as early as 60 (50 if you have a qualifying disability), though claiming early reduces the amount — survivor payments start at 71.5% at age 60 and rise to 100% at survivor full retirement age.

the birthday that costs six figures

Remarry before 60 and you permanently forfeit survivor benefits on the earlier record. Remarry at 60 or later and they're preserved. The cutoff is your 60th birthday, not the date of the marriage — meaning someone who remarries at 59 and 11 months gives up a benefit that someone remarrying a month later keeps. If you're near 60 and planning a wedding, this is worth a conversation with SSA first.

timing, and the earnings test

Claiming early permanently reduces your monthly check. Waiting increases it. There's no universally right answer — it depends on your health, your savings, and whether you need the income now.

One trap if you're still working: in 2026, earnings over $24,480 can temporarily reduce benefits before full retirement age. In the year you reach FRA the limit rises to $65,160, and after FRA the test disappears entirely. "Temporarily" is doing real work in that sentence — withheld amounts are generally credited back later — but it can produce an unpleasant surprise if you claim at 62 while working full time.

Benefits can also be taxable. Divorced-spouse benefits may be taxed if combined income exceeds $25,000 for single filers or $32,000 for joint filers.

how to actually check and claim

  1. Create a my Social Security account. It shows your own earnings record and retirement estimate — and lets you check that your earnings were reported correctly, which is worth doing regardless.
  2. Gather exact dates. Marriage date, divorce-final date, birth dates, and any remarriage dates. These dates decide eligibility under the 10-year rule, the 2-year rule, and the age-60 rules.
  3. Find the documents. Marriage certificate and divorce decree. If you don't have them, county clerk offices issue copies.
  4. Apply. Spouse and divorced-spouse claims can often start online within three months of turning 62. Survivor benefits cannot be applied for online — that's a phone call or a local office visit.
  5. Ask SSA to compare. They can tell you whether your own benefit or the spousal/survivor benefit is larger. Ask them to run both.

if you're helping a mother or grandmother

This is the article to forward. The women most likely to qualify for a divorced-spouse benefit — long first marriage, lower lifetime earnings, divorced decades ago — are also the least likely to have encountered this rule. Ten minutes and a marriage certificate can change a monthly income permanently.

the bottom line

Social Security is one of the few systems that quietly accounts for unpaid work and interrupted careers — and it does it through rules almost nobody explains out loud.

You don't need to master the whole program. You need to know that if you were married ten years, that marriage may still be worth money to you, no matter how it ended or how long ago.

Your next tiny step: find your marriage and divorce dates and count the months. That single number determines whether any of this applies to you — and it takes five minutes to answer.

questions people actually ask

does claiming on my ex's record reduce what they get?

No. Their benefit is completely unaffected, and so is their current spouse's. Social Security doesn't split one benefit between people — it pays each eligible person from the same earnings record.

This is the single biggest reason people don't claim: they assume they'd be taking something from someone. They wouldn't be.

will my ex find out?

No. There's no notification to your ex-spouse and no requirement that they consent or cooperate. You'll need the marriage certificate and divorce decree, not their signature.

If you don't know their Social Security number, SSA can often still locate the record using their name, date of birth, and place of birth.

what if i was married twice, both over 10 years?

You can generally have the higher benefit calculated, but you can only receive one — the larger of the options you qualify for.

Bring both sets of dates to SSA and ask them to compare records.

i remarried. did i lose this?

For divorced-spouse benefits on a living ex, remarriage generally ends eligibility. But if that later marriage ends by divorce, annulment, or death, eligibility on the earlier record can be restored.

For survivor benefits, remarrying at 60 or later doesn't affect them at all.

i barely worked. do i get nothing?

Not necessarily. That's exactly the situation spousal and survivor benefits exist for — they're based on someone else's earnings record, not yours.

Someone with very little work history of their own can still receive a meaningful benefit through a spouse or a qualifying ex-spouse.
