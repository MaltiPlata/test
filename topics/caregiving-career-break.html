the caregiving career break, priced

stepping back to care for someone is a real choice a lot of women make. this is the honest math on what it costs long after the caregiving ends — and the moves that soften it.

9 min readupdated august 2026topic: work

the short version

  • the cost is three to four times the salary you give up, because you also lose future raises, employer match, and social security credits.
  • one modeled example: a 30-year-old earning $50,000 who takes three years off loses about $415,000 over a lifetime — $150k in wages, $140k in lost wage growth, $125k in retirement.
  • a five-year pause in 401(k) contributions alone can mean roughly $346,000 less at retirement.
  • women get hit twice — once by the motherhood penalty, again by the parent-caregiving penalty in peak earning years.
  • the protective moves are small and boring: spousal IRA, credit in your own name, keep contributing something, get it in writing.

why this needs a number

The decision to step back usually gets framed as a monthly comparison: childcare costs $2,000, my take-home is $2,600, so working is barely worth it.

That framing is wrong, and it's wrong in a direction that costs women a fortune. It compares one year of cost against one year of income, when the actual loss compounds for thirty more years.

Center for American Progress research puts it plainly: workers can expect to lose up to three or four times their annual salary for each year out of the workforce. The losses stack because most people take these years relatively early, so a modest dent in income echoes across decades of earnings.

None of this means don't do it. Plenty of families make this choice deliberately and would make it again. It means make it with the real number in front of you, not the monthly one.

the actual math

CAP built a calculator that separates the loss into three parts. Their worked example: a 30-year-old woman earning $50,000 who takes three years of caregiving leave loses $150,000 in wages, plus $140,000 in lost wage growth over her career, plus $125,000 in retirement assets and benefits.

That's roughly $415,000 from three years — about 8x the annual salary, not 3x, because the second and third buckets keep growing long after she returns.

The retirement piece alone is startling. PensionBee modeled someone contributing $5,000 a year from age 25 to 62 and found that pausing contributions costs:

pause lengthretirement savings lost
1 year~$78,700
3 years~$221,000
5 years~$345,700

Those are contributions of $5,000 a year. Five years of pausing costs sixty-nine times what you skipped — because it's the compounding you lost, not the deposits.

and it isn't only childcare

Elder care hits later and harder. The Family Caregiver Alliance puts the total lifetime cost of caregiving for women at roughly $295,000 to $324,044, and AARP finds caregivers spend an average of 26% of their income — about $7,242 a year — on out-of-pocket caregiving costs. Nationally, unpaid family caregiving carries an estimated $107 billion annual opportunity cost.

why women pay twice

Here's the pattern that makes this specifically a women's issue rather than a parents' issue.

The first hit is the motherhood penalty, in your twenties or thirties. The second arrives in your fifties, when parents need care — often at midlife, when a lot of women are at their peak earning years, which is precisely when a career interruption does the most damage to lifetime earnings and Social Security calculations.

Men, in aggregate, don't show the same pattern. As one researcher put it, men "if anything, get a bit of a fatherhood bonus" — producing what she calls a double penalty for women: hit once in motherhood, hit again as a parental caregiver.

And it's currently getting worse, not better. Brookings observed labor force participation for women with children under five falling from 71% to 68% since September 2023, while participation among prime-age women overall held steady at 81%.

this is structural, not personal

If your retirement balance is smaller than your partner's or your peers', the reason is usually visible in your calendar history, not your character. Caregiving years are unpaid labor that the retirement system doesn't credit. Knowing that won't refill the account — but it should end the private theory that you were bad with money.

the moves that soften it

If you're considering a break, are in one, or are coming back from one, these are the levers. None require a lot of money.

  1. Open a spousal IRA. This is the most under-used tool in this entire situation. A working spouse can contribute to an IRA in the non-earning spouse's name — meaning retirement savings keep accruing in your own name during years with no paycheck. The account is legally yours regardless of what happens to the marriage.
  2. Contribute something, anything. The PensionBee numbers show the damage comes from the pause, not the amount. Even a fraction of your prior contribution keeps compounding alive.
  3. Keep credit in your own name. A card and history in your name only. Career breaks quietly erode credit files when everything is joint, and that surfaces later at exactly the wrong moment.
  4. Get the arrangement in writing. If you're stepping back within a marriage, a postnuptial agreement can address what the caregiving spouse is owed. This is the least romantic and most protective thing on this list.
  5. Track your ten years. Social Security credits and the 10-year marriage rule both hinge on time. Know where you stand on both.
  6. Negotiate the re-entry, not just the exit. Returning at your old salary means absorbing the entire lost wage-growth bucket. Benchmark against the market rate for your years of experience, not your last paycheck.

if you're doing the childcare-cost comparison

Compare against your career, not your current paycheck. The relevant question isn't "does daycare cost more than I take home this year" — it's "does daycare cost more than three to four times my salary per year out." Sometimes it still does, and stepping back is genuinely right. But the answer changes for a lot of families once the real number is on the table.

the bottom line

Caregiving is real work with real economic value that the retirement system simply doesn't count. That's a policy failure, not a personal one — but until the policy changes, the cost lands on individual women, and the only defense is seeing it early.

The good news is that the protective moves are small: an account in your name, a continued contribution, a written agreement. None of them require the break to be shorter or the love to be smaller.

Your next tiny step: if you're married and one of you earns less or nothing, find out whether you're eligible for a spousal IRA. It's the single highest-value fifteen minutes in this article.

questions people actually ask

is it ever worth stepping back financially?

Sometimes, yes — particularly when childcare costs approach or exceed the lower earner's take-home pay, when care needs are intensive, or when the non-financial value is what matters most to your family.

The point isn't that the answer is always no. It's that the comparison should be against three-to-four times your annual salary per year out, not against one year of take-home.

what's a spousal ira?

An IRA funded by a working spouse in the name of a spouse with little or no earned income. It requires filing jointly, and normal IRA contribution limits apply.

It's the main way to keep retirement savings growing in your own name during unpaid caregiving years — and the account belongs to you regardless of what happens to the marriage.

i already took years off. is it too late?

No. You can't recover the compounding you missed, but you can change the slope from here — catch-up contributions after 50 exist precisely for this, and negotiating your return salary against market rate rather than your old one recovers part of the wage-growth bucket.

does caregiving affect my social security?

Yes. Benefits are calculated from your highest 35 years of earnings, and years with no earnings count as zeros in that average.

This is also why spousal and survivor benefits matter so much for caregivers — they're calculated on someone else's record, which can be far larger than your own.

how do i bring this up with my partner?

Frame it as pricing a shared decision rather than protecting yourself from them. The family is choosing to buy caregiving with one person's career, and it's fair to know the price and decide together how to offset it.

Concrete asks: fund a spousal IRA, keep credit in both names separately, and put the arrangement in writing.
