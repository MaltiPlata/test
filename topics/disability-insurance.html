the grown-up stuff · insurance

the insurance nobody talks about

your paycheck funds every other financial plan you have. this is the coverage that keeps it coming if you can't work — and most people have far less of it than they think.

8 min readupdated august 2026topic: insurance

the short version

  • the SSA estimates more than 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will be out of work for a year or more due to a disabling condition before retirement.
  • 9 out of 10 people underestimate their own chance of becoming disabled — and most picture a car accident, when the leading cause is musculoskeletal (back, spine, joints).
  • only about 33% of US workers have long-term disability through an employer; roughly 39% have short-term.
  • policies typically replace 60–70% of income. individual own-occupation coverage runs roughly 2–4% of your income.
  • the phrase that matters most: “own occupation” vs “any occupation.” it decides whether you're covered if you can't do your job or only if you can't do any job.

why this is the coverage people skip

Life insurance gets discussed constantly. Disability insurance rarely does — despite being the more likely claim during working years.

The Social Security Administration estimates more than 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before retirement, and about 1 in 7 adults between 35 and 65 will face a disability lasting five years or longer.

Meanwhile 9 out of 10 individuals underestimate their own chances of becoming disabled. And the mental image is usually wrong — most people picture a car accident, when musculoskeletal disorders account for 29% of long-term disability claims: backs, spines, knees, hips, shoulders.

The financial math is stark: being totally disabled for six months to a year could wipe out a decade of savings. And your emergency fund was built for three to six months, not for an average claim lasting 34.6 months.

SSDI is not a substitute. The standard is strict, the process is slow, and the benefit is modest. At least 51 million working adults in the US have no disability insurance other than the basic coverage available through Social Security — which is closer to no coverage than most people assume.

Note also that workers' compensation only covers on-the-job injuries. Most disabilities aren't work-related — almost all short-term disability plans involve non-occupational coverage precisely because that's where the risk is.

the two words that decide everything

If you read only one section, make it this one.

"Own occupation" means you're covered if you can't perform your specific job. A surgeon with a hand tremor can't operate — covered, even if she could work as a consultant.

"Any occupation" means you're only covered if you can't perform any job you're reasonably suited for. The same surgeon might be denied because she could theoretically do something else.

The gap between those definitions is the difference between a policy that pays and one that doesn't. Own-occupation costs more — individual own-occupation coverage runs roughly 2% to 4% of your income — and it's the version worth having if your income depends on specific skills.

check your employer policy for this word

Many group policies are own-occupation for a limited period — often two years — then switch to any-occupation. That's exactly when a long claim would still be running; the average claim is nearly three years. Read the definition and the switch date before assuming you're covered.

why employer coverage often isn't enough

Group coverage through work is valuable and usually cheap or free. It's also frequently insufficient on its own, for four reasons:

  • It's capped. Often a percentage of base salary with a monthly maximum — which can badly under-cover higher earners or anyone with significant bonus or commission income.
  • It's taxable if your employer pays the premium. A "60% benefit" can land closer to 40% after tax. Conversely, an individual policy you pay for yourself pays a tax-free benefit — so 40–65% coverage can bring home closer to 70–80% of your usual after-tax paycheck.
  • It usually isn't portable. Leave the job, lose the coverage — and you may be less insurable by then.
  • The definition may weaken over time (see above).

Access is also unequal. BLS data shows long-term disability access ranging from 10% for service workers to 59% for management and professional workers — meaning the people with the least financial cushion often have the least coverage.

what to check before you buy

  1. Start with what you already have. Read your employer's STD and LTD summaries. Note the replacement percentage, the monthly cap, who pays the premium, and the occupation definition.
  2. Calculate the gap. Compare the after-tax benefit against your actual essential monthly expenses — not your income. That difference is what an individual policy needs to fill.
  3. Check the elimination period. How long you must be disabled before benefits begin — commonly 90 days. Longer waits mean cheaper premiums, and your emergency fund has to cover the gap.
  4. Check the benefit period. To age 65 or 67 is the meaningful version. Two- or five-year benefit periods cost less and cover less.
  5. Look for non-cancelable and guaranteed renewable — meaning they can't drop you or raise your premium as you age or your health changes.
  6. Ask about a residual/partial benefit — pays if you can work part-time or at reduced income, which is how many disabilities actually unfold.

buy it while you're healthy and young

Premiums are priced on age and health at purchase. A condition diagnosed next year can make coverage expensive or unavailable — and unlike most financial mistakes, this one can't be corrected later. It's the rare product where "I'll deal with it when I need it" is structurally impossible.

the bottom line

Every financial plan you have — the emergency fund, the retirement contributions, the mortgage — assumes your income keeps arriving. Disability insurance is the coverage on that assumption.

It's unglamorous, it's the thing people skip, and it's more likely to be claimed during your working life than the life insurance everyone talks about instead.

Your next tiny step: find your employer's benefits summary and search it for the words "own occupation." What you find there tells you whether you have real coverage or the appearance of it.

questions people actually ask

isn't social security disability enough?

Generally no. SSDI has a strict definition, a slow process, and a modest benefit — and at least 51 million working adults have nothing beyond it.

It's a floor, not a plan.

how much does it cost?

Individual own-occupation coverage typically runs about 2–4% of your income, varying with age, health, occupation, and location.

Group coverage through an employer is usually far cheaper or free, with the tradeoffs described above.

what's the difference between short and long term?

Short-term covers weeks to a few months and typically replaces around 60% of salary. Long-term picks up after and can run to retirement.

Most people need long-term more than they think, because the average claim lasts nearly three years.

does workers' comp cover me?

Only for injuries and illnesses arising from your job. Most disabilities aren't work-related — musculoskeletal conditions, cancer, and mental health conditions are common claim causes.

That's the gap disability insurance is designed to fill.

i work from a desk. do i really need this?

The leading cause of long-term disability claims is musculoskeletal — back, spine, and joint conditions — which are not occupation-specific.

Illness accounts for far more claims than accidents. Desk work lowers injury risk, not disability risk.
