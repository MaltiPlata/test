how to vet a financial advisor

the title means nothing — anyone can use it. what protects you is the legal standard they operate under and how they get paid, and both are free to look up before you ever sit down.

8 min readupdated august 2026topic: work

the short version

  • "financial advisor" is not a protected title. anyone can use it. what matters is their registration.
  • fiduciary means legally required to act in your best interest. suitability / Reg BI is a lower bar that leaves room for recommendations that pay them more.
  • fee-only = paid only by you. fee-based = paid by you and by commissions. the two words look alike on purpose.
  • you can verify anyone in about a minute on FINRA BrokerCheck and the SEC's IAPD — both free, both public.
  • the question to ask: "are you a fiduciary at all times with me, and will you put that in writing?" hesitation is the answer.

the title means nothing

There is no license required to call yourself a "financial advisor" or a "financial planner." Titles in financial services are marketing — the protection comes from three things underneath: the legal standard they're held to, who regulates them, and how they get paid.

All three are public record, free to check, and take about as long as reading a restaurant menu.

fiduciary vs. everyone else

This is the single most important distinction, and it's structural rather than moral. It isn't about whether someone is a good person — it's about whose side the person across the table is legally required to be on when the interests don't align.

Fiduciary standard. Registered Investment Advisers, generally under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940, must act in your best interest at all times.

Suitability / Regulation Best Interest. Brokers operate under a lower bar — a recommendation must be reasonably suitable or in your "best interest" at the point of recommendation. The SEC's 2019 Reg BI raised it somewhat, but there are carve-outs and the enforcement track record is mixed.

the dual-registration trap

Many professionals are dual-registered — meaning they can switch hats between fiduciary and suitability standards depending on which product they're recommending. That's legal and common. It also means "I'm a fiduciary" can be true in one meeting and not in the next. The precise question is: "Are you acting as a fiduciary for this specific recommendation, right now?"

fee-only vs. fee-based — one word, big difference

These two terms are nearly identical and mean opposite things, which is not an accident.

Fee-only means the advisor is compensated solely by you — no commissions, no kickbacks, no referral fees. Fee-based means they charge you and can earn commissions on what they sell you. As one plain summary puts it, the conflict is real: they may recommend an annuity that pays them a 5% commission when a simple index fund would serve you better.

the 60-second background check

Do this for anyone you're considering, no matter who referred them.

  1. FINRA BrokerCheck (brokercheck.finra.org). Shows registrations, firms they've been associated with, and disclosures — customer complaints, regulatory actions, terminations.
  2. SEC's IAPD (adviserinfo.sec.gov). Confirms whether a person or firm is registered as an investment adviser, and gives you the Form ADV.
  3. Read Form ADV Part 2A — the "brochure." Every RIA files it, and it spells out how the firm is compensated, what conflicts of interest exist, and what services are actually offered. Dull reading, most direct paper trail available.
  4. Verify credentials. CFP® status can be confirmed at cfp.net/verify. CFP, CFA, and ChFC signal education and competence — but only the RIA registration guarantees the fiduciary standard.

where to find fee-only fiduciaries

Three directories that pre-screen for this: NAPFA (fee-only fiduciaries, full vetting), Garrett Planning Network (hourly and project-fee advisors — good if you don't have assets to manage but want a one-time plan), and XY Planning Network (fee-only, often monthly-subscription, aimed at younger clients). Verify anyone you find there anyway — being in a directory isn't the same as passing your own check.

the questions, and the answers you want

  • "Are you a fiduciary at all times when working with me?" Then: "Is that in writing?" A real fiduciary answers both without hesitation. If you get "we act in a fiduciary capacity," that's a hedge, not a yes.
  • "Do you receive any compensation, in any form, from third parties for anything you recommend to me?" You want a clean no, or a specific, complete yes.
  • "How exactly are you paid, and what will this cost me in dollars in year one?" Percentage-of-assets, hourly, flat fee, or subscription — and the dollar figure, not just the percentage.
  • "What regulatory body oversees you, and can I look you up on BrokerCheck?" If someone can't tell you whether they're an investment adviser, a broker-dealer rep, or both, that itself is a red flag.
  • "What happens if I want to leave?" There should be no penalty. Assets transfer in kind, typically within a couple of weeks.

the free consultation that isn't advice

Many advisors offer complimentary consultations that look and feel like financial advice but are really sales presentations. Until you're in a formal, documented advisory relationship with a fiduciary, nothing said in that room is a binding plan — and nothing obligates you to buy.

do you even need one?

Honestly, often not yet. If your situation is a steady paycheck, a 401(k), and an index fund, you may be doing everything an advisor would tell you to do.

Where one earns their fee: equity compensation, self-employment, a divorce or inheritance, multiple states, a business, caring for aging parents, or the years right around retirement when the sequencing decisions get genuinely complex.

And if you want help without handing over assets, hourly and flat-fee planners exist — Garrett and XY are built for exactly that. You can buy three hours of advice, get a plan, and implement it yourself.

the bottom line

The financial industry's language is confusing in ways that reliably benefit the person selling. "Advisor," "fee-based," "acts in a fiduciary capacity" — each one sounds like the safe version of itself.

You don't have to decode all of it. You have to check two free databases and ask one question twice.

Your next tiny step: if you already work with someone, look them up on BrokerCheck tonight. You're not accusing anyone of anything — this is simply how these relationships are meant to be evaluated, and a genuine fiduciary expects it.

questions people actually ask

is every financial advisor a fiduciary?

No. Generally only Registered Investment Advisers are legally required to act in your best interest at all times. Brokers operate under Regulation Best Interest, a lower standard.

Many professionals are dual-registered and can move between the two depending on the product.

what's the difference between fee-only and fee-based?

Fee-only advisors are paid solely by you. Fee-based advisors are paid by you and can also earn commissions on products they sell.

The similarity of the two words is one of the more consequential naming choices in the industry.

how much should an advisor cost?

Common structures are a percentage of assets managed, an hourly rate, a flat project fee, or a monthly subscription. Ask for the dollar cost in year one, not just the percentage.

If you don't have significant assets, hourly or flat-fee planners are usually the better fit.

does a cfp mean they're a fiduciary?

CFP® professionals commit to a fiduciary duty when providing financial planning, and the credential requires real education and exams.

It's a strong signal, but still check the registration — the CFP verification tool at cfp.net and the SEC's IAPD answer different questions.

can i fire my advisor?

Yes, at any time, and there's typically no penalty for leaving an advisory relationship. Assets generally transfer in kind to a new custodian within a week or two.

Some insurance products like annuities have surrender charges — that's the product, not the advisor relationship.
