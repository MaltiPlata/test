the grown-up stuff · retirement

the account with three tax breaks

deductible going in, tax-free growth, tax-free coming out. no other account does all three. here's why it's a retirement account wearing a medical disguise.

8 min readupdated august 2026topic: retirement

the short version

  • an HSA is triple tax-advantaged: deductible contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical costs. no other common account does all three.
  • you must be enrolled in a qualifying high-deductible health plan (HDHP) — that's the gate.
  • the strategy most people miss: pay medical bills out of pocket, keep receipts, let the HSA invest, and reimburse yourself years later. there is no time limit.
  • open it early even with a token amount — only expenses incurred after the account exists can ever be reimbursed from it.
  • after 65 non-medical withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income with no penalty — so it behaves like a traditional IRA with a tax-free medical bonus.

the triple advantage, precisely

Every investment account gets taxed at one of three moments: when money goes in, while it grows, or when it comes out. Most accounts spare you one or two of those. An HSA spares you all three.

Roth IRAtaxedtax-freetax-free

*for qualified medical expenses. As one summary puts it, the 401(k) loses the third round and the Roth IRA loses the first — the HSA wins all three.

There's also no income limit on contributing, unlike a Roth IRA, and unspent money rolls over forever — HSAs are not FSAs, and the "use it or lose it" rule does not apply.

To be eligible you must be enrolled in a qualifying HDHP — for 2026, a minimum deductible of $1,650 self-only or $3,300 family, with out-of-pocket maximums not exceeding $8,300 and $16,600 — and you can't be enrolled in Medicare or claimed as someone's dependent.

verify the number before you contribute

You'll find slightly different 2026 figures circulating online, because limits are announced in advance and some articles quote earlier drafts. The IRS revenue procedure is the authority — check irs.gov or your HSA provider before maxing out, and note that California and New Jersey don't conform to the federal deduction, so residents there lose the first of the three advantages at the state level.

the receipt strategy

Here's the move that turns a medical account into a retirement account.

Most people use the HSA debit card at the pharmacy. The alternative: pay medical bills out of pocket, keep the receipt, and leave the HSA invested. Then reimburse yourself whenever you want — including decades later.

The rule that makes this work: there is no time limit on when you take the distribution. A $3,000 medical bill you paid out of pocket in 2026 can be reimbursed from your HSA in 2046 — and because the account grew tax-free for twenty years, you're effectively pulling tax-free money out of a compounded account.

the one rule that makes or breaks it

Open the account early, even with a token amount. The establishment date is a hard wall: only expenses incurred after the account exists can ever be reimbursed from it. An HSA opened this month starts the clock on every future receipt. And the receipts themselves are the strategy — one honest assessment: households that make it a boring automatic habit barely notice the effort; households that plan to organize it later usually don't. Photograph it, drop it in a folder, done.

Most providers let you invest once the balance passes a threshold, typically around $1,000. Money sitting in the cash portion of an HSA earns almost nothing — check whether yours is actually invested, because many people assume it is and it isn't.

what happens at 65

The account changes character at 65, in your favor.

Before 65: non-medical withdrawals are taxed as income plus a 20% penalty — steeper than the 10% on a traditional IRA. Don't treat it as a general savings account.

After 65: non-medical withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income with no penalty — the same treatment as a traditional IRA. Medical withdrawals remain fully tax-free.

So the worst case at 65 is that your HSA behaves exactly like a traditional IRA, and the best case is that it's tax-free. That's an unusually forgiving downside, and it's why "over-contributing" to an HSA is hard to regret.

One planning note: you can't contribute once you're enrolled in Medicare, and there are look-back rules around enrollment. Stop contributions before Medicare starts, or you can face penalties.

when an HSA is the wrong answer

The tax benefits are real, but they ride on a health-plan decision that has to stand on its own. An HDHP is not right for everyone.

Think carefully if: you have chronic conditions with predictable high costs, you're planning a pregnancy, you take expensive ongoing medications, or you can't comfortably cover the deductible from savings. Choosing a worse health plan to capture a tax break is a bad trade — the deductible is real money and it comes first.

Also worth knowing: some employers contribute to your HSA, which is free money on top of the tax treatment and can flip the math in favor of the HDHP. Check whether yours does before comparing plans on premium alone.

And the order of operations still holds: capture your full 401(k) match first, clear high-interest debt, then look at the HSA — which many planners put ahead of additional 401(k) contributions once the match is captured, precisely because of the third tax break.

the bottom line

The HSA is the best-kept ordinary secret in the tax code — a retirement account that most people spend at the pharmacy counter because of the word "health" in its name.

You don't have to run the full receipt strategy to benefit. Contributing, investing rather than leaving it in cash, and not spending it unless you need to captures most of the value.

Your next tiny step: if you have an HSA, log in and check whether the balance is actually invested or sitting in cash. That one check is worth more than everything else on this page for most people.

questions people actually ask

is an hsa the same as an fsa?

No, and the difference is significant. FSA funds are generally use-it-or-lose-it within the plan year and don't belong to you if you leave the job.

HSA funds roll over forever, are yours permanently, and can be invested.

what counts as a qualified medical expense?

A broad list including doctor visits, prescriptions, dental, vision, and many over-the-counter items. IRS Publication 502 is the reference.

Keep documentation for anything you reimburse — the burden of proof is on you if asked.

can i still contribute if i change jobs?

The account is yours and goes with you. You can keep contributing as long as you remain enrolled in a qualifying HDHP.

If your new plan isn't an HDHP, you keep the account and the money but stop contributing.

what if i never have big medical bills?

Then after 65 it works like a traditional IRA — withdraw for anything, taxed as ordinary income, no penalty.

Also worth noting that most people do have substantial medical costs in retirement, so the tax-free pool tends to get used.

should i max my hsa before my 401(k)?

Get your full employer 401(k) match first — that's an immediate return nothing beats. After that, many planners favor the HSA over additional 401(k) dollars because of the third tax break.

This depends on your tax situation and whether the HDHP is right for your health needs.
