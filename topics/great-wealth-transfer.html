the grown-up stuff · inheritance

$124 trillion is about to change hands

the largest wealth transfer in history is underway, and most of it is coming to women. inheriting money and being ready to run it are two different things.

8 min readupdated august 2026topic: life

the short version

  • $124 trillion is projected to change hands in the US by 2048 — and women are expected to receive roughly 70% of it.
  • about $54 trillion moves first between spouses, and 95% of surviving spouses are women. another $47 trillion goes to younger women directly.
  • by 2030, women are projected to control roughly two-thirds of US private wealth.
  • do nothing for 90 days. park it somewhere safe and boring. no decisions, no purchases, no advisors hired in week one.
  • inheritances arrive with tax and timing rules that vary by asset — an inherited IRA is not the same as an inherited house or a life insurance payout.

the scale of it

Cerulli Associates projects $124 trillion will change hands in the US through 2048 — $105 trillion to heirs and $18 trillion to charity. For scale, that exceeds total global GDP.

The gendered part is the striking part. Roughly $54 trillion moves first between spouses — and among that group, 95% of surviving spouses are women. Another $47 trillion is expected to pass to women in younger generations as inherited wealth.

Bank of America's analyst puts it simply: women will inherit about 70% of $124 trillion over the next 25 years. And McKinsey has projected that by 2030 roughly two-thirds of US private wealth will be held by women — the largest wealth transfer by gender in history.

money moving isn't power moving

The financial industry has greeted these projections as a historic correction. There's a sharp critique worth sitting with.

Money can transfer legally without power transferring culturally: a woman can own assets without inheriting the relationships, access, and authority to use them. The industry calls this a confidence gap and prescribes literacy workshops — the argument is that it's actually an authority gap, created by systems that made women responsible for the daily management of money while keeping them from long-term decisions until a death or divorce forced it.

The practical version: much of this wealth arrives at the worst possible moment — during grief, often to someone who wasn't in the meetings, holding relationships with advisors chosen by someone else.

what this means before it happens

If you're married or partnered, the single most protective thing is to be in the conversations now. Know where accounts are, who the advisors are, what the passwords are, and what the plan is. Not because anything is wrong — because the alternative is learning all of it in the two weeks after a funeral.

the first 90 days: do nothing

This is the most valuable advice in this article and the hardest to follow.

Park the money somewhere safe and boring and make no decisions for about three months. A high-yield savings account is fine. Not a purchase, not an investment, not a new advisor, not a loan to a relative.

Two reasons. First, grief is a terrible state for irreversible financial decisions, and inheritances almost always arrive attached to a loss. Second, the arrival of money reliably summons people with plans for it — some well-meaning, some not — and "I'm not making any decisions for 90 days" is a complete sentence that ends every one of those conversations.

Nothing you'd want to do with the money gets worse for waiting three months. Nearly everything you'd regret gets prevented by it.

the rules that vary by asset

"An inheritance" isn't one thing, and the type determines the tax and timing rules. As one planner puts it, whether it's an inherited 401(k), a life insurance payout, or liquid assets transferring to your name, there are implications for how much you should take out and when.

what you inheritthe thing to know
inherited traditional IRA/401(k)withdrawals are generally taxable; most non-spouse heirs face a 10-year drawdown window
inherited Roth IRAgenerally tax-free withdrawals, but distribution timing rules still apply
life insurance payoutgenerally not taxable income to the beneficiary
a house or investmentsoften receives a stepped-up cost basis at death — significant for capital gains
a spouse's accountsspouses have options non-spouses don't, including treating an IRA as their own

The stepped-up basis point is worth understanding because it's frequently missed: inherited assets are often valued as of the date of death, which can eliminate a large amount of embedded capital gain. Selling shortly after inheriting can therefore be far cheaper tax-wise than people assume.

inheritances can also arrive with debt

If the person who died didn't have life insurance, long-term care, or disability coverage, there may be debts left behind that erode the inheritance. Find out what's owed before planning around what's owned. Generally you don't personally inherit someone's debts — but the estate pays them first, which changes what actually reaches you.

after the 90 days

  1. Get a full inventory. Every account, every debt, every policy, every property. You can't plan around a number you don't have.
  2. Handle the boring urgent things first. High-interest debt, an emergency fund, and any tax obligations from the inheritance itself.
  3. Hire help on your terms. If you use an advisor, verify them yourself on BrokerCheck and the SEC's IAPD — and don't default to the inherited advisor simply because they were already there. That relationship was someone else's choice.
  4. Update your own documents. A significant inheritance changes your estate picture: will, beneficiary designations, and possibly a trust.
  5. Decide what it's for. Money without a purpose gets absorbed into daily spending and disappears. Naming the job — retirement, a house, a career change, giving — is what makes it durable.

the bottom line

An enormous amount of money is about to arrive in women's hands, much of it during the hardest weeks of their lives, and often to people who were deliberately kept at arm's length from the long-term decisions.

The preparation isn't complicated: be in the conversations now, and when it comes, wait ninety days before doing anything.

Your next tiny step: if you're partnered, ask this week where the accounts are and who the advisors are. Not a plan, not a meeting — just the list. That single conversation is the whole preparation.

questions people actually ask

do i pay tax on an inheritance?

It depends on the asset. Life insurance payouts are generally not taxable income; withdrawals from an inherited traditional IRA generally are.

A handful of states have their own inheritance taxes, and the federal estate tax is paid by the estate rather than the recipient. This is a good place for a one-time consultation with a tax professional.

what's the 10-year rule on inherited iras?

Under current rules, most non-spouse beneficiaries must generally empty an inherited IRA within 10 years of the original owner's death.

Spouses have more flexible options. The details have shifted with recent legislation, so confirm current rules before setting a withdrawal schedule.

should i keep my parent's financial advisor?

Not automatically. That relationship was chosen by someone else for someone else's situation.

Verify them on BrokerCheck and the SEC's IAPD, ask directly whether they're a fiduciary at all times, and treat it as hiring rather than inheriting.

what if the inheritance comes with debt?

Generally you don't personally inherit someone's debts — the estate pays creditors before distributing what's left.

But that reduces what reaches you, and jointly held debts can be different. Get clarity on what's owed before planning around what's owned.

how do i talk to my parents about this?

Frame it as wanting to honor their wishes rather than as asking about money. The questions that matter most are logistical: where are the documents, who are the advisors, what do you want to happen.
