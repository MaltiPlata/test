prenups & postnups

a fairness list you write while you still like each other. what it actually covers, what makes one enforceable, what it costs — and how to raise it without it sounding like you're planning an exit.

8 min readupdated august 2026topic: legal

the short version

  • a prenup is a contract about property, debt, and support if the marriage ends. it is not a prediction and not an insult.
  • it cannot decide child custody or child support — courts keep that authority no matter what you write.
  • the things that get prenups thrown out are procedural: rushed signing, hidden assets, and pressure. California, for example, requires the final version be presented at least 7 days before signing.
  • each person should have their own attorney. it's not always legally required, but a shared lawyer is one of the most common reasons an agreement gets challenged.
  • cost ranges widely — roughly $600–$1,000 for template-based work up to $1,000–$5,000+ attorney-drafted, depending on complexity and location.

what a prenup actually is

A prenuptial agreement is a contract two people sign before marrying that sets out what happens to property, debt, and financial support if the marriage ends — by divorce or by death.

Here's the part nobody says out loud: you already have a prenup. If you don't write one, your state's default rules apply — rules written by a legislature that has never met you, and that vary enormously depending on where you live. Nine states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin) treat most assets and debts acquired during marriage as community property, split differently than in the other forty-one.

So the real choice isn't "prenup or no prenup." It's "the default one, or the one you wrote."

a postnup is the same thing, later

A postnuptial agreement does the same job but is signed after you're already married. It's for when circumstances change — one of you starts a business, an inheritance arrives, one person steps back from a career to raise kids, or you simply never got around to a prenup. Later than ideal, dramatically better than never.

what it can and can't cover

Prenups are broad on money and firmly limited on children.

what stays separate property vs. becomes sharedchild custody arrangements
how debt brought in is treatedchild support amounts
spousal support / alimony termsanything illegal or unconscionable
business ownership and its growthpersonal/lifestyle clauses (often unenforceable)
inheritance and property rights at deathterms that would leave one spouse destitute

The child exclusion is not a loophole to find a way around — courts retain authority over custody and support because those belong to the child, not to the parents' contract. Any clause attempting to fix them is generally disregarded.

The most valuable thing a prenup does for many couples is quieter than asset protection: it clarifies debt. If one of you brings significant student loans or credit card debt into the marriage, a prenup can state plainly that it stays that person's responsibility.

what makes one actually hold up

Prenups get invalidated far more often for how they were signed than for what they said. Four procedural things matter most:

  1. It's in writing and properly executed. Oral prenups are unenforceable everywhere. Many states add requirements — notarization, witnesses, specific formalities.
  2. Full financial disclosure. Both people list assets, debts, and income honestly. Hiding an account is one of the surest ways to have the whole agreement torn up later.
  3. Nobody was rushed. Signing days before the wedding invites a claim of duress. California's Family Code requires the final agreement be presented at least seven days before signing, and courts elsewhere scrutinize last-minute agreements even without a statutory rule.
  4. It isn't unconscionable. An agreement so lopsided that it leaves one spouse with nothing tends not to survive review.

the mistake that undoes the most prenups

Sharing one lawyer. A single attorney cannot fully represent two people whose interests may diverge. Some states require independent counsel for particular terms — in California, waiving spousal support generally requires that the waiving party had their own attorney. Even where it's not mandatory, separate counsel is the strongest evidence that both people understood what they signed and chose it freely. If your partner's lawyer drafted it, get your own to review it. That review is far cheaper than the litigation it prevents.

what it costs

Cost varies more than most legal services, driven by complexity, location, and how much negotiating happens.

~$650

typical template-based agreement, simple finances

$1k–$3k

common attorney-drafted range

$5k+

complex assets, businesses, or high-cost metros

Two things push the number up sharply: disagreement (each additional negotiation session bills hourly) and geography (major metro attorneys commonly charge substantially more than those outside them). Rush jobs can carry premium fees too — and they're also the agreements courts look at most skeptically, so paying extra to hurry buys you a weaker document.

Templates can work for genuinely simple situations — two people, similar incomes, no business, no children from prior relationships. But because enforceability turns on state-specific procedure, a template that ignores your state's rules can fail exactly when you need it. At minimum, have an attorney in your state review it.

how to bring it up

The legal part is the easy part. The conversation is what stops most people.

The reframe that helps: a prenup is not a prediction of divorce, it's a financial conversation with a deadline. Couples who go through the process are forced to say out loud what they earn, what they owe, what they expect, and how they want to handle money together. Most couples never have that conversation at all — and financial conflict is a well-documented source of marital strain.

Practical guidance for raising it:

  • Early, not late. Months before the wedding, not weeks. This protects both the relationship and the enforceability.
  • Lead with the shared goal, not with your assets. "I want us to actually talk about money before we merge our lives" lands differently than "I want to protect what's mine."
  • Make it mutual. Both people list everything; both people get counsel. A prenup framed as one person's protection against the other is both worse for the relationship and more vulnerable in court.
  • Separate the document from the discussion. Have the money conversation first, then decide what to write down.

who this matters most for

Prenups get framed as a rich-person thing. In practice they matter most when there's asymmetry: one person owns a business, one brings significant debt, one has children from a previous relationship, one expects an inheritance, or one plans to step back from paid work to raise kids. That last one deserves emphasis — the person who reduces earnings for caregiving is often the most financially exposed if a marriage ends, and a prenup is one of the few tools that can address it in advance.

the process, step by step

  1. Have the money conversation. Before any lawyer, before any document. Incomes, debts, expectations, goals.
  2. Each person lists everything. Assets, debts, income, expected inheritances. Complete disclosure is a legal requirement, not a courtesy.
  3. Each person retains their own attorney. Family law, licensed in the state where you'll live.
  4. Draft and negotiate. Expect revisions. This is normal and is not a sign the relationship is in trouble.
  5. Sign well before the wedding. Weeks or months, not days — respect your state's waiting period if it has one.
  6. Store it safely and revisit it. Keep signed copies somewhere both of you can reach. Major life changes may call for an amendment or a postnup.

the bottom line

A prenup is a document about fairness written at the one moment when both people are most inclined to be fair to each other. That's the whole logic of doing it early.

It's also one of the few legal tools that specifically addresses the financial vulnerability created by unpaid caregiving work — which is exactly the kind of thing that goes unmentioned until it's too late to fix.

Your next tiny step: before any lawyer or template, each of you write down what you own, what you owe, and what you earn. Trade lists. That single exercise is the actual foundation, and plenty of couples find it valuable whether or not they ever sign anything.

questions people actually ask

does wanting a prenup mean i don't trust my partner?

The process requires full financial honesty from both people and an explicit conversation about money — which is close to the opposite of distrust.

What it does mean is that you'd rather decide these things together now, calmly, than have a court decide them later under the worst possible circumstances.

are prenups even enforceable?

Prenuptial agreements are recognized in all 50 states and D.C. But requirements vary — 29 states plus D.C. have adopted a version of the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act, and the rest apply their own rules.

Enforceability usually turns on procedure: writing, disclosure, voluntariness, timing, and fairness at signing. A prenup that's solid in one state can be vulnerable in another, which is why state-specific drafting matters.

can we write our own without lawyers?

Some states allow it if the writing and procedural requirements are met, and templates exist for simple situations.

The risk is that the rules that make it enforceable are state-specific and easy to miss — and you find out it failed at the worst possible time. At minimum, have an attorney in your state review it before signing.

what if we're already married?

Then you're looking at a postnuptial agreement, which does substantially the same job after the wedding.

Postnups can face additional scrutiny in some states because spouses already owe each other legal duties, so independent counsel matters even more here.

can a prenup cover custody of our future kids?

No. Courts retain authority over child custody and child support regardless of what a prenup says, because those rights belong to the child.

Clauses attempting to set them are generally disregarded — but the rest of the agreement can still stand.
