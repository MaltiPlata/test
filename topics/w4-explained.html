taxes · your paycheck

the w-4, explained

You filled this out on your first day, probably in a hurry, possibly guessing. It's the form that decides how much tax leaves every single paycheck for as long as you hold that job — and almost nobody looks at it again.

7 min readupdated august 2026topic: taxes

the short version

  • The W-4 doesn't set what you owe. It sets what gets prepaid from each paycheck.
  • A big refund means you overpaid all year — an interest-free loan to the government. Owing a lot means you underpaid.
  • The form hasn't used "allowances" since 2020. If someone tells you to "claim 1," they're describing a form that no longer exists.
  • The most common cause of an April surprise is a second income — a spouse's job, a side gig — because each employer withholds as if its paycheck is your only one.
  • You can hand in a new W-4 any time. You don't need a reason and you don't need to wait for January.

what withholding actually is

Your employer is required to send part of your pay to the IRS on your behalf, every payday, all year. That's withholding. By April you've already prepaid most of what you owe — the tax return is just the reconciliation.

Two outcomes, and both mean the estimate was off:

  • You get a refund. Too much was withheld. The government is returning your own money, without interest, after holding it for up to sixteen months.
  • You owe. Too little was withheld. You kept more per paycheck and now the balance is due at once.

Neither is a reward or a punishment. They're the same amount of tax, timed differently.

the reframe worth having

A $3,600 refund feels like a windfall. It's actually $300 a month that could have been in your emergency fund, paying down a credit card at 24%, or sitting in a high-yield savings account earning interest all year.

That said — if the refund is the only way you reliably save, keep it. A system that works beats an optimal one you won't follow. Just make that a choice rather than an accident.

the five steps, in plain language

The form was redesigned in 2020 and no longer uses allowances. Now it asks about actual dollars.

Step 1 — who you are

Name, address, Social Security number, filing status. Filing status matters more than people realize: single, married filing jointly, or head of household each use different withholding tables. Single parents especially should check this one — head of household uses a bigger standard deduction and better brackets than filing single, and it changes withholding too.

Step 2 — more than one income

This is the step that causes most April surprises, and the one most often skipped.

Each employer calculates withholding as though its paycheck is your only income. Two jobs paying $40,000 each will each withhold as if you earn $40,000 — but you earn $80,000, and you're in a higher bracket than either employer assumed. The same happens when both spouses work and neither W-4 accounts for the other.

Complete Step 2 if you have a second job or a working spouse. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator handles the math.

Step 3 — dependents

Where you claim the Child Tax Credit and the credit for other dependents. This directly reduces withholding, so leaving it blank when you do have children means overpaying every paycheck. The 2026 amounts are here.

Step 4 — everything else

Three optional lines that do a lot of work:

  • 4(a) other income — interest, dividends, retirement income, freelance income. Entering it here lets your job's withholding cover it, which can replace quarterly payments entirely.
  • 4(b) deductions — only if you itemize beyond the standard deduction.
  • 4(c) extra withholding — a flat dollar amount per paycheck. The simplest, bluntest lever on the form.

Step 5 — sign it

An unsigned W-4 isn't valid, and your employer will withhold at the highest rate.

the trick for anyone with side income

If you have a job and freelance work, you have two options for covering the tax on the freelance side: mail four quarterly payments, or raise the withholding at your job using line 4(c).

The second is usually easier, and it has a genuine advantage. The IRS treats withholding as paid evenly across all four quarters, even if you increase it in November.

why that matters

A quarterly payment is credited when you make it — miss April and you're penalized for April, even if you pay double in June. Withholding doesn't work that way. Increase it late in the year and the IRS treats it as though it was spread across the whole year.

It's the one legitimate way to retroactively fix an underpayment. If you realize in October that you're short, this is the fix. The full freelance tax picture →

when to redo it

You can submit a new W-4 whenever you want. Most people should after any of these:

  • You got married or divorced
  • You had a baby, or a child aged out of the Child Tax Credit at 17
  • You or your spouse started or ended a job
  • You started freelancing or picked up a side income
  • You got a significant raise
  • You owed more than expected — or got a refund larger than you'd like

The practical method: run the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov with a recent pay stub in hand. It'll tell you exactly what to put on the form. Then give the new W-4 to HR or payroll — not to the IRS.

Worth doing in early in the year if you can. Changes made in January spread across twelve paychecks; the same correction in October has to come out of two.

the bottom line

The goal isn't a big refund or a zero balance. It's no surprises — a number in April you already knew was coming.

If you've never revisited the W-4 you filled out on your first day, that's the fifteen-minute task. Especially if anything has changed since — and something almost always has.

questions people actually ask

should i claim 0 or 1?

Neither — that's the old form. Allowances were removed in the 2020 redesign, so there's nothing to "claim" anymore.

If your goal was more withheld per paycheck (the old "claim 0"), the current equivalent is entering a dollar amount in Step 4(c).

why did i owe money when nothing changed?

Usually a second income entered the picture — a spouse's job, a side gig, interest from a high-yield savings account, or a retirement withdrawal. Each source withholds only against itself, so the combined total lands in a higher bracket than any single employer assumed.

Step 2 or line 4(a) is where that gets corrected.

can my employer refuse to change my withholding?

No. You're entitled to submit a new W-4 and they're required to apply it, generally by the start of the first payroll period ending 30 or more days after you hand it in.

i'm married — should we file jointly and how does that affect the w-4?

Filing status and withholding are linked, and joint filing is the better outcome for most couples — though not all. The important part for the W-4 is that if you both work, at least one of you should complete Step 2, or you'll be under-withheld.

Filing status also affects other things entirely, like whether a spousal IRA is available to you.

does a bigger refund mean i did taxes right?

It means you overpaid throughout the year. It has nothing to do with whether your return was correct — the refund is just the difference between what you prepaid and what you owed.
